## 1. Scope of the result

I do not obtain a construction or a nonexistence proof. The partial result below rules out a class of possible constructions.

Fixing a vertex of a hypothetical graph produces 57 sets of 56 vertices, with a perfect matching between every pair of sets. After normalizing the labels using one of these sets, let \(\Gamma\leq S_{56}\) be generated by the matching permutations.

I prove:

> **Structural restriction.** Every \(\Gamma\)-invariant real subspace of the zero-sum permutation module \(\mathbb R^{56}_0\) has dimension divisible by \(5\). Consequently, \(\Gamma\) is primitive and nonsolvable.
>
> In particular, the matchings cannot all belong to a common solvable permutation group, nor can they all be translations in a group of order \(56\).

The argument is self-contained. I have not established whether this observation already appears in the literature.

## 2. The forced rooted structure and spectrum

Assume that \(G\) is a finite simple 57-regular graph of diameter \(2\) and girth \(5\). Fix a vertex \(v\), write
\[
N(v)=\{u_1,\ldots,u_{57}\},
\qquad
X_i=N(u_i)\setminus\{v\}.
\]

Each \(X_i\) has size \(56\). The absence of triangles and quadrilaterals makes these sets pairwise disjoint and disjoint from \(N(v)\cup\{v\}\). Diameter \(2\) implies that they exhaust the remaining vertices. Thus
\[
|V(G)|=1+57+57\cdot56=3250.
\]

Each \(X_i\) is independent. Moreover, for distinct \(i,j\), the edges between \(X_i\) and \(X_j\) form a perfect matching. Indeed, if \(x\in X_i\), then \(x\) is not adjacent to \(u_j\), since that would create a quadrilateral through \(v\). The pair \(x,u_j\) therefore has a unique common neighbor, which must belong to \(X_j\). Reversing \(i,j\) establishes the perfect-matching assertion.

More generally, adjacent vertices have no common neighbor, and distinct nonadjacent vertices have exactly one common neighbor. Hence the adjacency matrix satisfies
\[
A^2=56I-A+J. \tag{1}
\]
Since \(G\) is connected and regular, its eigenvalue \(57\) is simple, with eigenvector \(\mathbf 1\). On \(\mathbf 1^\perp\), equation (1) gives
\[
A^2+A-56I=0.
\]
The other eigenvalues are consequently \(7\) and \(-8\). Using \(\operatorname{tr}A=0\) gives
\[
\operatorname{Spec}(G)=
\{57^{(1)},\,7^{(1729)},\,(-8)^{(1520)}\}. \tag{2}
\]

## 3. Normalized matching permutations

Let \(\Omega\) be a set of size \(56\), and label \(X_1\) by \(\Omega\). Label every other \(X_i\) using its matching with \(X_1\): the neighbor in \(X_i\) of the vertex labeled \(a\) in \(X_1\) receives label \(a\).

For \(i\ne j\), define
\[
\pi_{ij}\in\operatorname{Sym}(\Omega)
\]
by declaring that the vertex labeled \(a\) in \(X_i\) is matched to the vertex labeled \(\pi_{ij}(a)\) in \(X_j\). Then
\[
\pi_{ji}=\pi_{ij}^{-1},
\qquad
\pi_{1i}=\pi_{i1}=\mathrm{id}.
\]
Set
\[
\Gamma=\langle\pi_{ij}:i\ne j\rangle.
\]

First, \(\Gamma\) is transitive on \(\Omega\). To see this, fix \(a\in\Omega\) and consider the vertex \(x\in X_1\) labeled \(a\). Its neighbor in \(X_2\) has label \(a\). For every \(b\ne a\), the vertex \(y\in X_2\) labeled \(b\) is nonadjacent to \(x\), so \(x,y\) have a unique common neighbor.

That common neighbor lies in some \(X_k\), with \(k\notin\{1,2\}\), and has label \(a\), by normalization. Thus
\[
\pi_{k2}(a)=b.
\]
In fact,
\[
\{a\}\ \cup\
\{\pi_{k2}(a):3\leq k\leq57\}
=\Omega, \tag{3}
\]
with all 56 displayed values distinct. In particular, every label belongs to the \(\Gamma\)-orbit of \(a\).

## 4. An invariant-subspace divisibility obstruction

Write
\[
\mathbb R^\Omega_0
=
\left\{z\in\mathbb R^\Omega:
\sum_{a\in\Omega}z(a)=0\right\}.
\]

> **Lemma.** If \(W\subseteq\mathbb R^\Omega_0\) is a \(\Gamma\)-invariant real subspace, then
> \[
> 5\mid\dim W.
> \]

**Proof.** Put \(d=\dim W\). Consider the subspace \(\widehat W\) of functions \(f:V(G)\to\mathbb R\) defined by
\[
f(v)=f(u_i)=0\quad(1\leq i\leq57),
\qquad
f|_{X_i}\in W\quad(1\leq i\leq57).
\]
Here every \(X_i\) is identified with \(\Omega\) by the normalized labeling. Thus
\[
\dim\widehat W=57d.
\]

The subspace \(\widehat W\) is \(A\)-invariant:

* At \(v\), the value of \(Af\) is zero.
* At \(u_i\),
  \[
  (Af)(u_i)=\sum_{a\in\Omega}f|_{X_i}(a)=0.
  \]
* On \(X_i\),
  \[
  (Af)|_{X_i}(a)
  =
  \sum_{j\ne i}f|_{X_j}\bigl(\pi_{ij}(a)\bigr),
  \]
  which belongs to \(W\), because \(W\) is invariant under the matching permutations.

Also, \(\widehat W\subseteq\mathbf1^\perp\). Therefore \(A|_{\widehat W}\) has only the eigenvalues \(7\) and \(-8\).

Relative to the decomposition
\[
\widehat W=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{57}W,
\]
all diagonal blocks of \(A|_{\widehat W}\) are zero, since every \(X_i\) is independent. Consequently,
\[
\operatorname{tr}(A|_{\widehat W})=0.
\]

If the multiplicities of \(7,-8\) on \(\widehat W\) are respectively \(a,b\), then
\[
a+b=57d,
\qquad
7a-8b=0.
\]
Solving,
\[
a=\frac{152d}{5},
\qquad
b=\frac{133d}{5}.
\]
Both are integers, so \(5\mid d\). \(\square\)

This is stronger than merely saying that certain group actions are impossible: it restricts every invariant real subspace of the matching group’s permutation representation.

## 5. Consequences for construction attempts

### 5.1. The matching group is primitive

Suppose that the transitive group \(\Gamma\) preserves a nontrivial partition of \(\Omega\) into \(m\) blocks.

Transitivity makes all blocks equally large, so
\[
m\mid56,
\qquad
1<m<56.
\]
The vectors that are constant on each block form a \(\Gamma\)-invariant subspace of dimension \(m\). Its intersection with \(\mathbb R^\Omega_0\) has dimension \(m-1\). The lemma therefore implies
\[
m\equiv1\pmod5. \tag{4}
\]

But the proper divisors of \(56\) greater than \(1\) are
\[
2,4,7,8,14,28,
\]
none of which is \(1\pmod5\). This contradicts (4). Thus \(\Gamma\) is primitive.

### 5.2. The matching group is nonsolvable

Here is the relevant elementary permutation-group fact, including its proof.

Suppose that a finite faithful primitive permutation group \(H\) is solvable. Choose a minimal nontrivial normal subgroup \(N\). Solvability and minimality imply that \(N\) is elementary abelian of order \(p^e\) for some prime \(p\): its derived subgroup must be trivial, and its characteristic primary and exponent subgroups force it to be elementary abelian.

The \(N\)-orbits form an \(H\)-invariant partition. Primitivity and faithfulness therefore force \(N\) to be transitive. An abelian transitive faithful permutation group is regular: an element fixing one point fixes every point by commutativity. Hence the permutation degree is
\[
|N|=p^e.
\]

Applying this to \(\Gamma\), its degree would have to be a prime power. Since
\[
56=2^3\cdot7
\]
is not a prime power, \(\Gamma\) cannot be solvable.

### 5.3. Common-group and translation models are excluded

These conclusions rule out both of the following ansätze:

* All matching permutations belong to a common solvable subgroup of \(S_{56}\).
* All matching permutations preserve a common nontrivial block system on the 56 labels.

The obstruction is not an artifact of the normalization. If, before normalization, all matching maps lie in a fixed permutation group \(K\), then normalization replaces them by maps of the form
\[
q_j^{-1}\pi_{ij}q_i,
\qquad q_i\in K.
\]
Thus the normalized maps still lie in \(K\).

In particular, suppose the labels form a group \(L\) of order \(56\), and every matching is left translation by an element of \(L\). The normalized matching group is then a transitive subgroup of the regular action of \(L\), and hence equals that regular action.

But a regular group of order \(56\) is imprimitive: a subgroup of order \(2\) exists, and its cosets give a nontrivial invariant partition. This contradicts primitivity. The same reasoning applies to right translations.

Thus even allowing a nonabelian group of order \(56\) does not rescue a common translation construction.

## 6. A consequence for actual automorphisms

The auxiliary group \(\Gamma\) is not generally an automorphism group of \(G\). Nevertheless, the argument also gives a genuine restriction on \(\operatorname{Aut}(G)\):

> **Corollary.** An automorphism fixing \(v\) and every vertex of \(N(v)\) is the identity. Equivalently, each vertex stabilizer acts faithfully on its 57 neighbors.

To prove this, let such an automorphism induce permutations \(\sigma_i\) on the labeled sets \(X_i\). Preservation of the matchings gives
\[
\sigma_j\pi_{ij}=\pi_{ij}\sigma_i.
\]
Since \(\pi_{1i}=\mathrm{id}\), all \(\sigma_i\) equal one permutation \(\sigma\), and \(\sigma\) centralizes \(\Gamma\).

The centralizer of a transitive permutation group is semiregular: an element of the centralizer fixing one point fixes all points. Its orbits also form a block system for the original group.

Consequently, if \(C_{S_{56}}(\Gamma)\) were nontrivial, primitivity would force it to be transitive. A transitive group commuting with \(\Gamma\) then forces \(\Gamma\) itself to be regular: any point stabilizer in \(\Gamma\) fixes the entire orbit of the commuting group. This is impossible for a primitive group of degree \(56\), as above.

Therefore the centralizer is trivial, so \(\sigma=\mathrm{id}\), proving the corollary.

## 7. What remains unresolved

The obstruction still permits large primitive nonsolvable matching groups. For example, the natural zero-sum module of \(S_{56}\) is irreducible of dimension \(55\), which satisfies the divisibility condition. Indeed, from a nonzero invariant vector with unequal coordinates, taking its difference with a suitable transposition produces a multiple of \(e_i-e_j\); the permutation images of these differences span the entire zero-sum module.

Thus the invariant-subspace condition alone cannot exclude the symmetric group. Nor would a large auxiliary matching group imply that \(G\) is highly symmetric: compositions of matching maps on the label set need not extend to graph automorphisms.

The missing step is still global: either construct matching permutations satisfying all triangle- and quadrilateral-exclusion conditions simultaneously, or prove that no unrestricted family can satisfy them. The argument above eliminates imprimitive and solvable common-group constructions, but does not cover the remaining primitive nonsolvable case.
