# A complete special case and a separator restriction

Graphs below are finite and simple. A toroidal graph is one embeddable in the orientable torus.

I do not obtain a proof or counterexample to the full conjecture. The main partial result is the following stronger version of its triangle-free case.

## 1. The triangle-free case

**Theorem 1.** Every connected triangle-free toroidal graph of minimum degree at least four has a Hamilton cycle. Indeed, every edge belongs to a Hamilton cycle.

More generally, the same conclusion holds for a connected simple graph of minimum degree at least four admitting a **cellular torus embedding with no triangular faces**, even if it has nonfacial triangles.

Thus 4-connectivity is unnecessary in this special case.

### 1.1. Euler’s formula forces a flat quadrangulation

First suppose \(G\) is triangle-free, and fix a torus embedding. Write
\[
n=|V(G)|,\qquad m=|E(G)|.
\]
We initially do not assume that the embedding is cellular.

Take a closed regular neighbourhood \(N\) of the embedded graph, and let
\[
R_1,\ldots,R_f
\]
be the components of the closure of the complement of \(N\). Each \(R_i\) is a compact surface with nonempty boundary, so \(\chi(R_i)\le 1\). Since \(N\) deformation-retracts onto \(G\), additivity of Euler characteristic gives
\[
0=\chi(\mathbb T^2)=n-m+\sum_{i=1}^f\chi(R_i).
\]
Consequently,
\[
m-n\le f. \tag{1}
\]

Every boundary walk has length at least four. Simplicity excludes loops; a facial walk cannot immediately reverse an edge at a vertex of degree at least two; and a closed walk of length three in a simple graph is a triangle. Counting the two sides of every edge therefore gives
\[
2m\ge 4f. \tag{2}
\]
On the other hand, minimum degree at least four gives
\[
m\ge 2n. \tag{3}
\]

Combining (1) and (2),
\[
m-n\le f\le \frac m2,
\]
so \(m\le 2n\). Equality must hold throughout. It follows that:

* every vertex has degree exactly four;
* every \(R_i\) has Euler characteristic one, hence is a disk;
* every facial boundary walk has length four.

Thus the embedding is cellular and quadrangular. Its face boundaries are genuine 4-cycles: a repeated vertex in a length-four closed walk in a simple graph would force an immediate edge reversal.

For the theorem’s more general, cellular version, the same argument applies directly. The only use of triangle-freeness was to exclude triangular facial walks.

### 1.2. Every such quadrangulation is a square-grid quotient

We need the following structural fact.

**Lemma 2.** Let \(G\) have a cellular embedding on the torus in which every vertex has degree four and every face is a quadrilateral. Then there is a finite-index subgroup
\[
\Lambda\le \mathbb Z^2
\]
such that \(G\), with its embedding, is the quotient of the standard square grid by the translations in \(\Lambda\).

**Proof.**
Give each face the metric of a Euclidean unit square, gluing corresponding edges isometrically. At each vertex exactly four square corners meet, so the total angle is \(2\pi\). The resulting metric on the torus is therefore flat everywhere.

Its universal cover is a complete simply connected flat surface, hence isometric to \(\mathbb R^2\). One can see this by developing adjacent squares into the plane: development is path-independent on the simply connected cover, and completeness makes the resulting local isometry a covering of \(\mathbb R^2\), hence an isometry. The lifted faces form the standard unit-square tiling.

Deck transformations preserve both orientation and this tiling. An orientation-preserving Euclidean isometry is a translation or a rotation; a nonidentity deck transformation has no fixed point, so it cannot be a rotation. All deck transformations are therefore translations. Since they preserve the tiling’s vertex set, their translation vectors lie in \(\mathbb Z^2\).

The quotient has finitely many vertices, so these vectors form a finite-index subgroup \(\Lambda\le\mathbb Z^2\). ∎

Set
\[
A=\mathbb Z^2/\Lambda,\qquad
a=(1,0)+\Lambda,\qquad b=(0,1)+\Lambda.
\]
The graph now has vertex set \(A\), with edges
\[
x\sim x\pm a,\qquad x\sim x\pm b.
\]
Because the graph is simple and 4-regular, these four neighbours are distinct. In particular,
\[
\operatorname{ord}(a)\ge 3,\qquad \operatorname{ord}(b)\ge 3. \tag{4}
\]

### 1.3. An explicit Hamilton-cycle construction

Let
\[
r=\operatorname{ord}(a),\qquad
s=[A:\langle a\rangle].
\]
The quotient \(A/\langle a\rangle\) is cyclic, generated by the image of \(b\). Its cosets can therefore be listed as
\[
\langle a\rangle,\ b+\langle a\rangle,\ldots,(s-1)b+\langle a\rangle.
\]

For each \(0\le j<s\), the \(a\)-edges give a cycle
\[
C_j=(jb,\ jb+a,\ldots,jb+(r-1)a,\ jb).
\]
These cycles are vertex-disjoint and together span \(G\). Between consecutive cycles there are matching edges
\[
jb+ia\ \sim\ (j+1)b+ia
\qquad (0\le i<r,\ 0\le j<s-1).
\]
Thus \(G\) contains a spanning copy of \(C_r\square P_s\). No direct-product decomposition of \(A\) is being assumed: any twist at the final torus seam is simply unused.

We merge the row cycles successively. Suppose the current cycle contains precisely the vertices of \(C_0,\ldots,C_j\). Choose an unused \(a\)-edge \(xy\) in row \(j\), and let
\[
x'=x+b,\qquad y'=y+b.
\]
Delete the two edges \(xy\) and \(x'y'\), and add
\[
xx',\qquad yy'.
\]
This joins the current cycle and \(C_{j+1}\) into one cycle.

The choices can be made explicitly by alternating between the row-edge positions
\[
\{jb,jb+a\}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\{jb+a,jb+2a\}.
\]
These are distinct because \(r\ge3\). Consequently, the edge chosen in an intermediate row differs from the one removed in the preceding splice.

After \(s-1\) splices, the resulting cycle is Hamiltonian.

Moreover, each row loses at most two \(a\)-edges. Since \(r\ge3\), the resulting Hamilton cycle contains an \(a\)-edge. Translations of \(A\) are graph automorphisms and act transitively on the \(a\)-edges. Hence **every \(a\)-edge** belongs to a Hamilton cycle. Interchanging \(a\) and \(b\) proves the same assertion for every \(b\)-edge.

These are all the edges, proving Theorem 1. ∎

In particular, every triangle-free 4-connected toroidal graph satisfies the conjecture.

## 2. A necessary cut condition for the full class

The following argument applies even when triangles are present.

**Proposition 3.** Every 4-connected toroidal graph is 1-tough: whenever \(G-S\) has at least two components,
\[
c(G-S)\le |S|.
\]

**Proof.**
Let
\[
s=|S|,\qquad c=c(G-S)\ge2,
\]
and denote the components of \(G-S\) by \(D_1,\ldots,D_c\).

Every \(D_i\) has at least four distinct neighbours in \(S\). Otherwise deleting its neighbourhood, of size at most three, would separate it from another component, contradicting 4-connectivity.

Contract each \(D_i\) to one vertex. Delete edges within \(S\), loops, and duplicate edges. The resulting graph \(H\) is a simple bipartite toroidal graph with bipartition
\[
S,\ \{d_1,\ldots,d_c\}.
\]
Hence
\[
|V(H)|=s+c,\qquad |E(H)|\ge4c.
\]
The bipartite toroidal Euler bound gives
\[
|E(H)|\le2|V(H)|.
\]
This bound also holds for disconnected graphs, by applying the connected bound to each component; components of order at most two are immediate.

It follows that
\[
4c\le2(s+c),
\]
and therefore \(c\le s\). ∎

Two consequences are worth recording.

1. For every independent set \(I\),
   \[
   |I|\le \frac{|V(G)|}{2}.
   \]
   Thus the simplest bipartition-imbalance obstruction to Hamiltonicity cannot occur.

2. If \(c(G-S)=|S|\), then equality holds throughout the proof. Every component of \(G-S\) has exactly four distinct neighbours in \(S\), and its bipartite incidence minor satisfies
   \[
   |E(H)|=2|V(H)|.
   \]
   However, this does **not** give a minimum-degree-four condition at the vertices of \(S\). It therefore does not automatically put \(H\) into the square-grid class of Theorem 1.

### Sharpness and an explicit edge obstruction

The cut bound is sharp. To specify a torus embedding of \(K_{4,4}\), take the square-grid quotient by
\[
\Lambda=\langle(4,0),(-2,2)\rangle.
\]
Its vertex group can be written as \(\mathbb Z_4\times\mathbb Z_2\), with
\[
a=(1,0),\qquad b=(1,1).
\]
The four neighbours of each vertex are precisely the four vertices of opposite first-coordinate parity, so the quotient graph is \(K_{4,4}\). Deleting either bipartition class leaves four components.

Now add a diagonal between the two vertices of one bipartition class on a quadrilateral face. The resulting graph remains 4-connected and toroidal, but the new edge belongs to **no** Hamilton cycle. Indeed, the other bipartition class remains an independent set containing exactly half the vertices; every Hamilton cycle must therefore alternate between the two classes.

This graph is still Hamiltonian, since it contains \(K_{4,4}\). It is an obstruction only to extending the stronger **every-edge** conclusion, not to the original conjecture.

## 3. Where the argument stops

There is a precise numerical distinction between the solved flat case and the remaining case.

For a cellular torus embedding of a simple graph of minimum degree at least four, let \(t\) be the number of triangular faces. Euler’s formula gives
\[
\sum_{v\in V(G)}(d(v)-4)
+
\sum_{F}(\ell(F)-4)=0.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
\boxed{
t=
\sum_{v\in V(G)}(d(v)-4)
+
\sum_{\ell(F)\ge5}(\ell(F)-4).
}
\tag{5}
\]

When \(t=0\), every term on the right is nonnegative, so all vanish. This is exactly the 4-regular quadrangulation situation, where the square-grid quotient and cycle-splicing construction apply.

When triangular faces are present, equation (5) permits higher-degree vertices and longer faces. The square-grid structure no longer follows. The separator inequality does not repair this: component contraction can lose the internal routing information needed to lift a spanning cycle.

Thus the rigorous conclusions are:

* the conjecture holds, constructively, for triangle-free graphs;
* more generally, an embedding without triangular faces suffices;
* every 4-connected toroidal graph satisfies the necessary 1-toughness condition;
* any counterexample must have a triangular face in every cellular torus embedding.

No argument above eliminates that remaining triangular-face case, so the full conjecture is neither proved nor disproved here.
