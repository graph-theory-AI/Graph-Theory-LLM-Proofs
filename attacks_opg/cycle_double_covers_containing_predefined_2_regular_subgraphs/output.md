# Partial results

I do not obtain a proof or a counterexample to the full conjecture. Below are an integral special case, a small-cut reduction, and a fractional extension theorem. The integral special case includes some non-3-edge-colourable graphs.

No claim is made that these partial results are new. I do not rely on the supplied summaries of the recent papers.

For cut reductions, it is convenient to allow loopless multigraphs, with two parallel edges regarded as a cycle. All conclusions apply in particular to simple graphs.

## 1. An integral class satisfying the conjecture

The two operations used below are:

- A **2-edge sum**: delete one edge from each of two disjoint cubic graphs, then join their four free ends across the two graphs in pairs.
- A **3-edge sum**: delete one vertex from each of two disjoint cubic graphs, then join their three free ends across the two graphs according to a bijection.

Only connected bridgeless resulting graphs are considered.

Let \(\mathcal K\) be the class generated by connected 3-edge-colourable cubic graphs and the Petersen graph under these two operations.

**Theorem 1.**  
If \(G\in\mathcal K\), and \(S\) is a 2-regular subgraph such that \(G-E(S)\) is connected, then \(G\) has a cycle double cover containing every component of \(S\).

We prove the two base cases and the closure assertions.

### 1.1. A local identity for cubic cycle double covers

Suppose a family of cycles covers every edge of a cubic graph twice. At a vertex with incident edges \(a,b,c\), let \(t_{ab}\) count the cycles using the pair \(a,b\), and define \(t_{ac},t_{bc}\) similarly. Then
\[
t_{ab}+t_{ac}=2,\qquad
t_{ab}+t_{bc}=2,\qquad
t_{ac}+t_{bc}=2.
\]
Consequently,
\[
t_{ab}=t_{ac}=t_{bc}=1. \tag{1}
\]

Thus every incident edge-pair is used exactly once.

In particular, a cycle cannot occur twice in such a family: that would use one of its edge-pairs twice at each of its vertices. Therefore the constructions below, although conveniently described as indexed families, produce sets of distinct cycles as required in the question.

### 1.2. The 3-edge-colourable case

This case does not need the connectivity hypothesis on \(G-E(S)\).

**Proposition 2.**  
Every 2-regular subgraph of a 3-edge-colourable cubic graph is contained in a cycle double cover.

**Proof.**  
Let \(M_1,M_2,M_3\) be the three colour classes, and put
\[
F_i=G-M_i.
\]
Each \(F_i\) is a spanning 2-factor, and every edge belongs to exactly two of the \(F_i\).

Set
\[
R_i=F_i\triangle S,
\]
where symmetric difference is taken on edge sets. Each \(R_i\) is even. Since the ambient graph is cubic, every nonzero degree in \(R_i\) is two, so its nonempty components are cycles.

Take all components of
\[
S,\ R_1,\ R_2,\ R_3.
\]
For \(e\notin E(S)\), membership is unchanged by symmetric difference, so \(e\) occurs twice. For \(e\in E(S)\), toggling its membership in all three \(F_i\) changes its number of occurrences from two to one; adding \(S\) restores the number to two.

This is a cycle double cover containing \(S\). Distinctness follows from (1). \(\square\)

Here the four displayed subgraphs may be disconnected; the actual cover consists of their connected cycle components.

### 1.3. The Petersen graph

**Proposition 3.**  
The Petersen graph satisfies the conjecture.

**Proof.**  
A useful general bound is the following. If \(G\) has \(n\) vertices and \(s=|V(S)|=|E(S)|\), then connectivity of \(G-E(S)\) gives
\[
\frac{3n}{2}-s\ge n-1,
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
s\le \frac n2+1. \tag{2}
\]
For the Petersen graph, \(n=10\), so \(s\le6\). Its girth is five. Thus a nonempty admissible \(S\) is a single cycle of length five or six.

#### A prescribed 5-cycle

Let the prescribed cycle be
\[
C=(a_0,a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4,a_0),
\]
with indices modulo five.

Girth five implies that the five neighbours of \(C\) outside \(C\) are distinct: two attachments at the same outside vertex would create a triangle or a 4-cycle. Denote these neighbours by \(b_i\), where \(a_ib_i\) is an edge. Each \(b_i\) has two neighbours outside \(C\). Again by girth five, neither can be \(b_{i-1}\) or \(b_{i+1}\). Consequently the outside edges are exactly \(b_ib_{i+2}\).

For each \(i\), define the pentagon
\[
P_i=(a_i,a_{i+1},b_{i+1},b_{i+3},b_i,a_i).
\]
Then
\[
\{C,P_0,P_1,P_2,P_3,P_4\}
\]
is a cycle double cover:

- each edge \(a_ia_{i+1}\) occurs in \(C\) and \(P_i\);
- each spoke \(a_ib_i\) occurs in \(P_{i-1}\) and \(P_i\);
- each inner edge occurs in exactly two of the \(P_i\).

#### A prescribed 6-cycle

Write the prescribed cycle as
\[
C=(0,1,2,3,4,5,0).
\]
It has no chord, by girth five. Deleting its vertices leaves four vertices and three edges. Since \(G-E(C)\) is connected, these four vertices induce a tree.

That tree cannot be a path \(u-v-w-z\). Indeed, the two neighbours of \(u\) on \(C\) must be opposite on \(C\), to avoid a cycle of length at most four. The unique neighbour of \(v\) on \(C\) is adjacent on \(C\) to one of those two vertices, producing a 4-cycle through \(u,v\).

The outside tree is therefore a claw. Label its centre \(w\) and its leaves \(x,y,z\), so that the remaining edges are
\[
wx,wy,wz,\qquad
x0,x3,\ y1,y4,\ z2,z5.
\]
The following five cycles form the required cover:
\[
\begin{aligned}
&C,\\
&(x,0,1,y,4,5,z,2,3,x),\\
&(w,y,1,2,z,w),\\
&(w,x,3,4,y,w),\\
&(w,z,5,0,x,w).
\end{aligned}
\]
Every edge of \(C\) occurs once more outside \(C\). Each of the six edges joining \(C\) to \(x,y,z\) occurs in the 9-cycle and one pentagon. Each of \(wx,wy,wz\) occurs in two pentagons.

The empty prescribed subgraph, if allowed, is covered by the first construction. This exhausts the possibilities. \(\square\)

## 2. Gluing across small edge cuts

The prescribed cycles can be transported through both standard small-cut reductions.

**Lemma 4.**  
Suppose \(G\) is a connected bridgeless cubic graph and \(G-E(S)\) is connected.

1. Across a 2-edge cut, complete each side by one new edge.
2. Across a 3-edge cut, complete each side by one new degree-three vertex.

There are naturally induced admissible prescribed subgraphs on the completed sides. If both completed sides have cycle double covers containing those prescribed subgraphs, then so does \(G\).

### Proof for a 2-edge cut

Let
\[
\delta(A)=\{a_1b_1,a_2b_2\},
\]
where \(a_1,a_2\in A\) and \(b_1,b_2\notin A\).

Every cycle meets a cut evenly. Thus \(S\) uses either zero or two of these cut edges. It cannot use both, because then \(G-E(S)\) would be disconnected. Hence every component of \(S\) lies wholly on one side.

Complete the two sides by new edges \(a_1a_2\) and \(b_1b_2\). The completed graphs are loopless bridgeless cubic graphs. For example, an original cycle through a cut edge becomes a cycle through the new edge; the same observation shows that every internal edge remains on a cycle. The endpoints of either new edge are distinct, since otherwise the original graph would have a bridge.

Let \(S_A,S_B\) be the components of \(S\) on the respective sides. Their edge-deleted complements are connected: project paths of \(G-E(S)\) to a side, replacing excursions through the other side by the new edge.

In a cycle double cover of a completed side, the new edge lies in exactly two cycles. Deleting that edge from those two cycles gives two paths between its endpoints. Pair the two paths on the \(A\)-side with the two paths on the other side. For each pair, join them using the two original cut edges.

This creates two simple cycles. All other cover cycles are retained. Every internal edge keeps its multiplicity two, and each cut edge is used by both new cycles. No prescribed cycle was removed, because the prescribed cycles avoid the new edges. \(\square\)

### Proof for a 3-edge cut

Let
\[
\delta(A)=\{a_1b_1,a_2b_2,a_3b_3\}.
\]
Complete \(G[A]\) by a vertex \(v_A\) adjacent to \(a_1,a_2,a_3\), and complete the other side analogously by \(v_B\).

Again \(S\) uses either zero or two cut edges.

- If it uses none, retain the prescribed cycles lying on each side.
- If it uses two, say edges 1 and 2, exactly one component \(C\) of \(S\) crosses the cut. Its portion on the \(A\)-side is a path from \(a_1\) to \(a_2\). Close that path through \(v_A\), and do the analogous operation on the other side.

These completed prescribed subgraphs are 2-regular. Their complements are connected by projecting paths of \(G-E(S)\) to the completed sides. In the crossing case, the third cut edge is the sole surviving connection between the two sides, so each side of \(G-E(S)\) is itself connected.

As before, the completed cubic graphs are bridgeless: any original cycle meets the cut in zero or two edges and projects to a cycle in a completed side.

By (1), a cycle double cover of the \(A\)-side contains exactly one cycle through each pair
\[
\{v_Aa_i,v_Aa_j\},\qquad 1\le i<j\le3.
\]
Deleting \(v_A\) leaves a path \(P_A^{ij}\) from \(a_i\) to \(a_j\). Define \(P_B^{ij}\) similarly.

For each pair \(i,j\), join \(P_A^{ij}\) and \(P_B^{ij}\) using original cut edges \(i,j\). The result is a simple cycle; the two paths have disjoint vertex sets because they lie on opposite sides of the cut. Retain all cycles avoiding the added vertices.

Every internal edge is still covered twice. Cut edge \(i\) occurs in precisely the two newly formed cycles whose index-pairs contain \(i\).

If a prescribed cycle crossed the cut through edges 1 and 2, its two completed portions are exactly the cycles selected for the pair \(\{1,2\}\). They therefore glue back into the original prescribed cycle. All other prescribed cycles are retained. \(\square\)

Propositions 2 and 3, followed by repeated application of Lemma 4, prove Theorem 1.

### Consequence for a minimum counterexample

In the formulation allowing \(S=\varnothing\), a counterexample of minimum order has:

- no 2-edge cut;
- no nontrivial 3-edge cut;
- no proper 3-edge-colouring;
- underlying graph different from the Petersen graph.

The first two assertions follow because both completed sides are smaller admissible instances.

Temporarily allowing parallel edges does not weaken this reduction. A double edge separates its two endpoints from the rest by a 2-edge cut, unless the whole graph consists of two vertices joined by three edges; that exceptional graph is 3-edge-colourable. Thus a minimum counterexample in the multigraph formulation is automatically simple.

In a bridgeless cubic graph, a side of a 3-edge cut can be a tree only when it is a single vertex. A side of a 2-edge cut cannot be a tree. Consequently the small-cut conclusions amount to **cyclic 4-edge-connectivity**.

If one excludes the empty graph from the definition of a 2-regular subgraph, the positive results above are unchanged; the minimum-counterexample statement here is for the formulation also including \(S=\varnothing\).

## 3. The fractional relaxation holds without non-separation

There is a general fractional analogue considerably stronger than what the conjecture assumes.

A **fractional cycle double cover** assigns nonnegative weights \(w_D\) to cycles so that
\[
\sum_{D\ni e} w_D=2
\]
for every edge \(e\).

**Theorem 5.**  
Let \(G\) be a connected bridgeless cubic graph and let \(S\) be any 2-regular subgraph. There is a fractional cycle double cover in which every component of \(S\) has weight exactly one.

In particular, connectivity of \(G-E(S)\) is unnecessary for fractional feasibility.

**Proof.**  
Use the standard perfect-matching polytope theorem: the convex hull of perfect-matching incidence vectors is described by nonnegativity, the degree equations, and the odd-cut inequalities.

The vector
\[
x_e=\frac13\qquad(e\in E(G))
\]
satisfies these conditions. The degree equations follow from cubicity. For an odd vertex set \(U\), the cut \(\delta(U)\) has odd size. Since \(G\) is bridgeless, that size is at least three, so
\[
x(\delta(U))=\frac{|\delta(U)|}{3}\ge1.
\]

There are therefore perfect matchings \(M_1,\dots,M_t\) and nonnegative numbers \(\lambda_i\) such that
\[
\sum_i\lambda_i=1,
\qquad
\sum_i\lambda_i\mathbf 1_{M_i}
=\frac13\mathbf 1_{E(G)}.
\]
Put \(F_i=G-M_i\). Then
\[
\sum_i\lambda_i\mathbf 1_{F_i}
=\frac23\mathbf 1_{E(G)}.
\]

For each \(i\), decompose
\[
R_i=F_i\triangle S
\]
into its cycle components. Give each such component weight \(3\lambda_i\), adding weights when the same cycle occurs more than once. Give every component of \(S\) weight one.

For \(e\notin E(S)\), its total weight is
\[
3\sum_{i:e\in F_i}\lambda_i=2.
\]
For \(e\in E(S)\), it belongs to \(R_i\) exactly when it belongs to \(M_i\), so its total weight is
\[
1+3\sum_{i:e\in M_i}\lambda_i=1+1=2.
\]

Finally, a component of \(S\) cannot itself be a component of an \(R_i\). At every vertex of \(S\), the spanning 2-factor \(F_i\) uses at least one of the two incident \(S\)-edges; hence \(R_i\) cannot use both. Thus every prescribed component has weight exactly one. \(\square\)

This is the fractional version of the symmetric-difference construction in Proposition 2. The difference is that a convex combination of perfect matchings need not consist of three colour classes with coefficient \(1/3\).

## 4. Why fractional feasibility alone is insufficient

There is a concrete integrality obstruction if the non-separation hypothesis is dropped.

Consider the Petersen graph in its usual representation as an outer pentagon, an inner pentagon, and five matching spokes. Let \(S\) be the union of the two pentagons, and let \(M\) be the spoke matching.

There is **no integral cycle double cover containing both pentagons**.

Indeed, the prescribed pentagons already use, at every vertex, the pair of incident \(S\)-edges. By (1), every remaining cover cycle must alternate between edges of \(S\) and edges of \(M\).

Such a cycle uses an even number of spokes, because each spoke changes between the two pentagons. It uses at most five spokes, and it cannot use two, since that would give a 4-cycle. Therefore every remaining cycle uses exactly four spokes.

But the five spokes must each be covered twice, giving ten spoke occurrences in total. Ten cannot be a sum of fours, a contradiction.

This is **not** a counterexample to the conjecture: \(G-E(S)=M\) is disconnected. It does show that the fractional theorem cannot be rounded without using an additional structural hypothesis.

## 5. What remains open in this attack

The argument proves the conjecture for \(\mathcal K\), and reduces a minimum counterexample to a cyclically 4-edge-connected, non-3-edge-colourable graph other than the Petersen graph.

The missing step is integral rather than fractional. Theorem 5 supplies nonnegative cycle weights satisfying every required edge equation, including weight one on every prescribed component. What is not proved is that connectivity of \(G-E(S)\) forces those weights to admit an integral replacement.

Thus there is neither a complete proof nor an admissible counterexample here.
