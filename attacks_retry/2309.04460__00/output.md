## 1. Scope of the result

Let \(f(n)\) be the maximum average degree of a properly edge-coloured simple graph on \(n\) vertices without a rainbow cycle. The supplied question asks whether the known \(O(\log n\log\log n)\) upper bound can be improved to \(O(\log n)\).

I do not prove this in general. The partial result below handles a class of **noncommuting** colour systems, including the Cartesian powers of the properly \(3\)-coloured \(K_{3,3}\) appearing in the previous attempt. In this class, I obtain sharp logarithmic bounds:

- for arbitrary colour matchings, a sharp maximum-degree bound
  \[
  \Delta(G)\le 2\log_3 n;
  \]
- when every colour class is a perfect matching, a sharp bound
  \[
  \overline d(G)=\Delta(G)\le 3\log_6 n.
  \]

The number of blocks in the decomposition is unrestricted. This is important: the bounds do **not** acquire a factor equal to the number of blocks.

The arguments and sharpness constructions are verified below. I do not rely on the previous attempt’s common-complement assertion.

## 2. The additional algebraic hypothesis

For each colour \(c\), define an involution \(\sigma_c\) on \(V(G)\): it swaps the endpoints of every colour-\(c\) edge and fixes vertices not incident with that colour.

Suppose the colours have a partition
\[
B_1\sqcup\cdots\sqcup B_q
\]
with the following properties.

1. **Different blocks commute:** if \(a\in B_i\), \(b\in B_j\), and \(i\ne j\), then
   \[
   \sigma_a\sigma_b=\sigma_b\sigma_a.
   \tag{2.1}
   \]

2. **Rotations within a block commute:** for each \(i\), the subgroup generated by
   \[
   \{\sigma_a\sigma_b:a,b\in B_i\}
   \]
   is abelian.
   \tag{2.2}

Condition (2.2) does not require the individual involutions in \(B_i\) to commute. Fixing \(b\in B_i\), it is equivalent to requiring the permutations
\[
\tau_c=\sigma_c\sigma_b,\qquad c\in B_i,
\]
to commute. Moreover,
\[
\sigma_b\tau_c\sigma_b=\tau_c^{-1}.
\]
Thus each block generates a group of generalized-dihedral form, possibly with degeneracies.

Write
\[
d_i(v)=|\{c\in B_i:\text{\(v\) is incident with a colour-\(c\) edge}\}|.
\]
Properness gives \(d(v)=\sum_i d_i(v)\).

### Theorem

Assume \(G\) has no rainbow cycle and satisfies (2.1)–(2.2).

**(a)** For every vertex \(v\),
\[
\prod_{i=1}^q
\binom{d_i(v)+1}{\left\lfloor(d_i(v)+1)/2\right\rfloor}
\le n.
\tag{2.3}
\]
Consequently,
\[
\Delta(G)\le 2\log_3 n.
\tag{2.4}
\]

**(b)** If every colour class is a perfect matching, put \(k_i=|B_i|\) and \(k=\sum_i k_i\). Then
\[
\prod_{i=1}^q
2\binom{k_i}{\lfloor k_i/2\rfloor}
\le n,
\tag{2.5}
\]
where the blocks are nonempty. Consequently,
\[
\overline d(G)=k\le 3\log_6 n.
\tag{2.6}
\]

Both constants in (2.4) and (2.6) are attained for infinitely many \(n\), within their respective classes.

## 3. A fixed-point consequence of rainbow-cycle avoidance

The following observation is the bridge between graph theory and the permutation calculations.

### Lemma 1

If \(c_1,\ldots,c_\ell\) are distinct colours, then
\[
\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma_{c_\ell}\cdots\sigma_{c_1})
=
\bigcap_{j=1}^{\ell}\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma_{c_j}).
\tag{3.1}
\]

#### Proof

The inclusion from right to left is immediate.

Suppose the product fixes \(v\). Apply its factors successively, starting at \(v\). Delete any stationary steps. The remaining steps, if any, form a nonempty closed walk whose edge colours are all distinct. Such a walk contains a rainbow cycle, a contradiction.

Therefore every step was stationary. All intermediate vertices were \(v\), so every factor fixes \(v\). ∎

Two consequences will be used:

- a nonempty word in distinct colours incident with \(v\) cannot fix \(v\);
- if all colour classes are perfect matchings, a nonempty word in distinct colours has **no fixed point anywhere**.

The second statement also holds for every conjugate of such a word.

## 4. An elementary coding calculation

Consider one block, and temporarily denote its involutions by
\[
s_0,s_1,\ldots,s_{d-1}.
\]
Set
\[
\tau_j=s_js_0,\qquad \tau_0=1.
\]
The \(\tau_j\) commute, and \(s_0\) inverts each of them. For a subset \(S\), write
\[
\tau_S=\prod_{j\in S}\tau_j.
\]

The identities
\[
s_as_b=\tau_a\tau_b^{-1}
\tag{4.1}
\]
and
\[
s_{a_1}s_{b_1}\cdots s_{a_p}s_{b_p}s_c
=
\left(\prod_{j=1}^p\tau_{a_j}\right)
\left(\prod_{j=1}^p\tau_{b_j}^{-1}\right)\tau_c s_0
\tag{4.2}
\]
allow us to encode many permutations whose pairwise quotients are words in distinct colours.

Here is the precise bookkeeping. Let \(A,B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d-1\}\) be disjoint, and put \(\delta=|A|-|B|\).

- The permutation \(\tau_A\tau_B^{-1}\) is a word in distinct \(s_j\)'s whenever \(\delta\in\{-1,0,1\}\). Balance the two sides by adding index \(0\) to the smaller side if necessary, and use (4.1).
- The permutation \(\tau_A\tau_B^{-1}s_0\) is a word in distinct \(s_j\)'s whenever \(\delta\in\{0,1,2\}\). For an odd alternating word, the positive side must have one more index than the negative side. Add index \(0\) to the positive side when \(\delta=0\), or to the negative side when \(\delta=2\), and use (4.2).

The first word is empty only when \(A=B=\varnothing\). The second is always nonempty.

### 4.1. A code for partial matchings

For \(d\ge1\), set
\[
t=\left\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\right\rfloor,\qquad I=\{1,\ldots,d-1\}.
\]
Consider the following indexed list:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal A_d={}&
\{\tau_S:S\subseteq I,\ |S|\in\{t-1,t\}\}\\
&\cup
\{\tau_Ts_0:T\subseteq I,\ |T|\in\{t,t+1\}\}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.3}
\]
Out-of-range subset sizes contribute nothing.

For any two different indices in this list, the quotient of the corresponding permutations is a **nonempty word in distinct \(s_j\)'s**:

- within either line, the subset sizes differ by at most one, so the even-word calculation applies;
- from the first line to the second, \(|T|-|S|\in\{0,1,2\}\), so the odd-word calculation applies;
- reversing a quotient reverses its distinct-letter word.

The number of indices is
\[
\begin{aligned}
|\mathcal A_d|
&=\binom{d-1}{t-1}+2\binom{d-1}{t}+\binom{d-1}{t+1}\\
&=\binom{d+1}{t+1}
=\binom{d+1}{\lfloor(d+1)/2\rfloor}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.4}
\]
These are initially indexed lists, not an assumption that all listed permutations are distinct. The fixed-point argument below establishes the needed injectivity.

For \(d=0\), take the one-element list \(\{1\}\).

### 4.2. A larger code for perfect matchings

For \(d\ge1\), put \(h=\lfloor d/2\rfloor\). Consider
\[
\mathcal P_d=
\{\tau_S,\ \tau_Ss_0:
 S\subseteq\{0,\ldots,d-1\},\ |S|=h\}.
\tag{4.5}
\]
Its number of indices is
\[
|\mathcal P_d|=2\binom d{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}.
\tag{4.6}
\]

For two different indices, the quotient is either

1. a nonempty word in distinct \(s_j\)'s, or
2. \(s_0us_0\), where \(u\) is a nonempty word in distinct \(s_j\)'s.

To check this, first cancel the common subset indices. Quotients of the same parity are even words; after deleting index \(0\), the two remaining subset sizes differ by at most one.

For a quotient of opposite parity, the normal form is
\[
x=\tau_A\tau_B^{-1}s_0,
\]
where \(A,B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d-1\}\) are disjoint and
\[
|A|-|B|\in\{-1,0,1\}.
\]
The cases \(0\) and \(1\) were covered above. In the remaining case,
\[
s_0xs_0=\tau_B\tau_A^{-1}s_0,
\]
whose size difference is \(1\), so it is a nonempty distinct-letter word. This covers every case.

## 5. Proof of the theorem

### 5.1. Arbitrary colour matchings

Fix \(v\). In each block retain only the \(d_i(v)\) colours incident with \(v\), and form the list \(\mathcal A_{d_i(v)}\). Conditions (2.1)–(2.2) persist after this restriction.

Choose one indexed permutation \(w_i\) from each list and consider
\[
(w_1\cdots w_q)(v).
\tag{5.1}
\]
I claim that different tuples of indices give different vertices.

Suppose tuples \((w_i)\) and \((w_i')\) give the same vertex. Since permutations from different blocks commute,
\[
\left(\prod_i (w_i')^{-1}w_i\right)(v)=v.
\tag{5.2}
\]
For each differing block, its factor is a nonempty word in distinct colours from that block. Equal indices contribute the identity. Concatenating these words gives a nonempty word with no colour repeated, because the blocks are disjoint.

Every colour in this word is incident with \(v\). This contradicts Lemma 1. Thus (5.1) is injective, proving (2.3).

Let
\[
L(d)=\binom{d+1}{\lfloor(d+1)/2\rfloor}.
\]
Direct calculation gives
\[
L(0)=1,\qquad L(1)=2,\qquad L(d+2)\ge3L(d)\quad(d\ge0).
\]
Hence
\[
L(d)\ge3^{d/2}.
\]
Applying this to (2.3),
\[
n\ge\prod_iL(d_i(v))
\ge3^{\sum_i d_i(v)/2}
=3^{d(v)/2}.
\]
This proves (2.4).

### 5.2. Perfect colour matchings

Now every vertex is incident with every colour. Use the larger list \(\mathcal P_{k_i}\) in block \(i\).

As before, a collision between two tuples would give
\[
g=\prod_i (w_i')^{-1}w_i
\]
fixing a vertex \(v\).

For each differing block, write its factor as either \(u_i\) or
\[
r_i u_i r_i,
\]
where \(r_i\) is the reference involution of that block and \(u_i\) is a nonempty distinct-colour word. Because different blocks commute, all these separate conjugations can be combined:
\[
g=RUR^{-1},
\tag{5.3}
\]
where \(R\) is the product of the reference involutions for the conjugated factors, and \(U\) is the concatenation of all the \(u_i\)'s.

The word \(U\) is nonempty and has no repeated colour. Equation (5.3) would make \(U\) fix \(R^{-1}(v)\). This contradicts the perfect-matching consequence of Lemma 1.

Thus the endpoint map is injective, proving (2.5).

For completeness, set
\[
P(d)=2\binom d{\lfloor d/2\rfloor},\qquad d\ge1.
\]
We have
\[
P(d)\ge6^{d/3}.
\tag{5.4}
\]
Indeed, the cases \(d=1,2,3,4\) follow from \(P(d)=2,4,6,12\), and for \(d\ge3\),
\[
\frac{P(d+2)}{P(d)}
\ge\frac{10}{3}>6^{2/3}.
\]
Consequently, (2.5) gives
\[
n\ge6^{(\sum_i k_i)/3}.
\]
Since the graph is \(k\)-regular with \(k=\sum_i k_i\), this proves (2.6). ∎

## 6. Sharpness

### 6.1. Sharpness of the maximum-degree bound

Take the path \(P_3\), giving its two edges different colours. The two matching involutions generate a dihedral group, and their rotation subgroup is cyclic.

Form the \(t\)-fold Cartesian power, tagging each colour by its coordinate. Different coordinate blocks commute, so the hypotheses hold. The graph has
\[
n=3^t,\qquad \Delta=2t=2\log_3 n.
\]

It has no rainbow cycle: projection of a putative rainbow cycle onto a coordinate, after deleting stationary steps, would give a nonempty closed walk with distinct colours in \(P_3\), which is impossible.

Thus (2.4) is sharp.

### 6.2. Sharpness with perfect matchings

Here is an independent check of the previous attempt’s \(K_{3,3}\) construction.

Index both parts by \(\mathbb Z_3\), and colour \(xy\) by \(x+y\pmod3\). Each colour is a perfect matching. The graph has no rainbow cycle because it is bipartite and uses only three colours.

For a reference colour \(0\), the products \(\sigma_c\sigma_0\) act as translations by \(c\) on both parts. Thus the rotations commute, although distinct matching involutions do not.

In the \(t\)-fold Cartesian power, use coordinate-tagged colours. Its coordinate blocks satisfy (2.1)–(2.2), all colours are perfect matchings, and
\[
n=6^t,\qquad \overline d=3t=3\log_6 n.
\]
The projection argument again rules out a rainbow cycle.

Therefore (2.6) is sharp. In base-two notation, its constant is
\[
\frac{3}{\log_2 6}\approx1.16056.
\]

## 7. Why this does not settle Question 10.1

The algebraic decomposition is genuinely additional, even for perfect-matchings colourings.

For example, take two copies of \(\mathbb Z_5\) and three matchings given by
\[
i\longleftrightarrow i,\qquad
i\longleftrightarrow i+1,\qquad
i\longleftrightarrow p(i),
\]
where
\[
(p(0),p(1),p(2),p(3),p(4))=(2,4,0,1,3).
\]
These matchings are pairwise edge-disjoint. The resulting graph is properly \(3\)-coloured, cubic and bipartite, hence has no rainbow cycle.

Nevertheless, it admits no partition satisfying (2.1)–(2.2):

- each pair of matching involutions fails to commute—the corresponding products have orders \(5,6,6\);
- hence (2.1) forces all three colours into one block;
- the rotation permutations include inverses of the shift \(i\mapsto i+1\) and of \(p\), which do not commute.

So rainbow-cycle avoidance does not imply the structure used in the proof.

The established partial results are therefore
\[
\boxed{\Delta(G)\le2\log_3 n
\quad\text{under (2.1)–(2.2),}}
\]
and, sharply for perfect colour matchings,
\[
\boxed{\overline d(G)\le3\log_6 n.}
\]
No argument here bounds arbitrary noncommuting matching systems by \(O(\log n)\), and no superlogarithmic lower construction is obtained. The unrestricted necessity of the \(\log\log n\) factor is not decided.
