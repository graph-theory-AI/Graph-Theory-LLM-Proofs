# A partial result directly at degree 4

I do not resolve the stated existence question. I set aside the previous degree-6 approach and give an independent argument concerning 4-regular graphs themselves.

The result shows that, at sufficiently large girth, the fractional-coloring relaxation lies strictly below 3. Thus any graphs sought in the problem would exhibit an integrality gap: ordinary chromatic number 4 despite fractional chromatic number less than 3.

All graphs below are finite and simple.

## 1. Statement

A **fractional coloring** assigns nonnegative weights \(\lambda_A\) to independent sets \(A\), subject to
\[
\sum_{A\ni v}\lambda_A\ge 1
\qquad\text{for every vertex }v.
\]
The fractional chromatic number \(\chi_f(G)\) is the minimum of \(\sum_A\lambda_A\).

**Theorem.** Every 4-regular graph \(G\) of girth at least \(34\) satisfies
\[
\boxed{\chi_f(G)\le \frac{1875}{628}<3.}
\]
More precisely, there is a probability distribution on independent sets \(J\) of \(G\) such that
\[
\Pr(v\in J)\ge \frac{628}{1875}
=\frac13+\frac1{625}
\qquad\text{for every }v\in V(G).
\tag{1}
\]

The distribution will be generated by an explicitly specified randomized rule of radius \(16\). Consequently, the result also gives the weighted independence bound
\[
\max_{A\text{ independent}}\sum_{v\in A}w_v
\ge
\frac{628}{1875}\sum_{v\in V(G)}w_v
\tag{2}
\]
for every choice of nonnegative vertex weights.

The proof has three ingredients:

1. a local approximation to random greedy independent sets;
2. a positive probability of a two-for-one improvement;
3. a local rule making sufficiently many such improvements simultaneously.

## 2. A local independent set of density almost \(1/3\)

Assign independent uniform random variables
\[
U_v\in(0,1)
\]
to the vertices. All ties have probability zero.

### 2.1 Random greedy on the infinite 4-regular tree

The random greedy independent set processes vertices in increasing order of \(U_v\), accepting a vertex if no previously accepted neighbor is present.

On an infinite tree, this can be defined recursively through lower-priority neighbors. It is almost surely well-defined: the probability that a specified vertex begins a strictly decreasing-priority path of length \(r\) is at most
\[
\frac{4\cdot 3^{r-1}}{(r+1)!},
\tag{3}
\]
which tends to zero. Local finiteness then rules out an infinite dependency chain almost surely.

Consider the rooted infinite tree in which each vertex has three children. Let \(b(t)\) be the probability that its root has not been accepted before time \(t\). Conditional on the root arriving at time \(s\), it is accepted precisely when none of its three children has been accepted before \(s\). Before that arrival, the three descendant processes are independent. Therefore
\[
b(t)=1-\int_0^t b(s)^3\,ds.
\]
Thus
\[
b'(t)=-b(t)^3,\qquad b(0)=1,
\]
and hence
\[
b(t)=(1+2t)^{-1/2}.
\]

At the root of the infinite 4-regular tree, there are four such branches. Its probability of acceptance is consequently
\[
\int_0^1 b(t)^4\,dt
=
\int_0^1(1+2t)^{-2}\,dt
=
\frac13.
\tag{4}
\]

### 2.2 Making the rule local

For an integer \(R\ge1\), call a vertex \(v\) **certified** if there is no path
\[
v=x_0,x_1,\ldots,x_R
\]
with
\[
U_{x_0}>U_{x_1}>\cdots>U_{x_R}.
\]

Define \(I_R\) as follows:

- exclude every uncertified vertex;
- for a certified vertex \(v\), run greedy on the induced radius-\(R\) ball around \(v\), and include \(v\) if this local greedy computation accepts it.

This is a radius-\(R\) rule.

It also always produces an independent set. Indeed, if \(v\) is certified, every vertex on which its greedy decision depends is reachable by a strictly decreasing-priority path of length less than \(R\). All those dependencies lie inside the ball, and are closed under taking lower-priority neighbors. Thus the local decision agrees with the ordinary greedy decision on the whole finite graph. In particular, \(I_R\) is a subset of the global greedy independent set.

Every strict local minimum of \(U\)—a vertex whose priority is smaller than all its neighbors’ priorities—belongs to \(I_R\).

If \(G\) is 4-regular and its radius-\(R\) balls are trees, the local rule has the same distribution as on the infinite 4-regular tree. By (3) and (4),
\[
\Pr(v\in I_R)\ge
\frac13-\delta_R,
\qquad
\delta_R:=\frac{4\cdot3^{R-1}}{(R+1)!}.
\tag{5}
\]

We will use \(R=12\).

## 3. A safe local two-for-one improvement

The next construction applies to any independent set \(I\) in a triangle-free graph of maximum degree at most 4. No maximality assumption on \(I\) is needed.

A neighbor \(u\) of \(c\in I\) is **private to \(c\)** if
\[
N(u)\cap I=\{c\}.
\]
Let \(C\subseteq I\) be the set of vertices having at least two private neighbors.

Assign a second collection of independent uniform marks \(\eta_v\), independent of all previous randomness. For each \(c\in C\), let \(P(c)\) consist of the two private neighbors of \(c\) having the smallest \(\eta\)-marks.

Because the graph is triangle-free, the two vertices of \(P(c)\) are nonadjacent. Therefore
\[
(I\setminus\{c\})\cup P(c)
\]
is an independent set with one more vertex than \(I\). We must arrange simultaneous replacements without introducing edges between different added pairs.

For a vertex \(z\notin I\), define its **owner** \(o(z)\) when it has exactly one neighbor in \(I\); that neighbor is \(o(z)\).

For \(c\in C\), set
\[
D(c)=
\left\{
o(z):
u\in P(c),\
z\in N(u)\setminus\{c\},\
o(z)\text{ is defined}
\right\}
\setminus\{c\}.
\tag{6}
\]
Each of the two vertices of \(P(c)\) has at most three neighbors other than \(c\), so
\[
|D(c)|\le6.
\tag{7}
\]

Now assign a third independent collection of uniform marks \(\xi_v\), and select
\[
S=
\{c\in C:\xi_c<\xi_d\text{ for every }d\in D(c)\}.
\tag{8}
\]
Define
\[
I^+=(I\setminus S)\cup\bigcup_{c\in S}P(c).
\tag{9}
\]

### Lemma 1

The set \(I^+\) is independent, and
\[
|I^+|=|I|+|S|.
\tag{10}
\]
Conditional on \(I\) and the \(\eta\)-marks,
\[
\mathbb E|I^+|
\ge |I|+\frac{|C|}{7}.
\tag{11}
\]

### Proof

The pairs \(P(c)\) are pairwise disjoint: a vertex private to one member of \(I\) cannot be private to another.

Every added vertex in \(P(c)\) has \(c\) as its only neighbor in \(I\), and \(c\) is removed. There are no edges within a pair \(P(c)\), by triangle-freeness.

Suppose an edge joins \(u\in P(c)\) to \(z\in P(d)\), where \(c\ne d\). Since \(z\) is private to \(d\), equation (6) gives \(d\in D(c)\). Similarly, \(c\in D(d)\). Selecting both centers would require
\[
\xi_c<\xi_d
\quad\text{and}\quad
\xi_d<\xi_c,
\]
which is impossible. This covers every possible edge in \(I^+\), proving independence.

The added pairs are disjoint and lie outside \(I\), so (10) follows. Finally, conditional on \(I\) and \(\eta\), the set \(D(c)\) is fixed and independent of \(\xi\). Hence
\[
\Pr(c\in S\mid I,\eta)
=\frac1{1+|D(c)|}\ge\frac17.
\]
Linearity of expectation proves (11). ∎

### Locality

Given the membership indicators of \(I\):

- determining \(P(c)\) requires information within distance 2 of \(c\);
- determining \(D(c)\), and whether \(c\in S\), requires information within distance 3;
- determining whether a vertex belongs to \(I^+\) requires information within distance 4.

Thus, when \(I=I_R\), the resulting \(I^+\) is a radius-\((R+4)\) randomized rule.

The use of all potential owners in \(D(c)\), rather than only other eligible centers, is intentional: it gives both the bound of six competitors and this locality guarantee.

## 4. Enough vertices admit an improvement

We now lower-bound the probability that a vertex belongs to \(C\) when \(I=I_R\).

Fix a vertex \(v\) whose radius-4 ball is a tree. Let \(M_v\) be the event that \(v\) is a strict local minimum of \(U\). Since \(v\) has four neighbors,
\[
\Pr(M_v)=\frac15.
\tag{12}
\]
On this event, \(v\in I_R\).

Root the radius-4 ball at \(v\). For a vertex \(z\) at distance 2 from \(v\), let \(F_z\) be the event that at least one of its three children is a strict local minimum in the whole graph.

We first calculate \(\Pr(F_z)\). Conditional on \(U_z=t\), a specified child \(w\) of \(z\) is a local minimum with probability
\[
s(t)=\int_0^t(1-x)^3\,dx
=\frac{1-(1-t)^4}{4}.
\]
The three child events are conditionally independent, since their other neighbors lie in disjoint branches. Thus
\[
\begin{aligned}
1-\Pr(F_z)
&=\int_0^1(1-s(t))^3\,dt\\
&=\frac1{64}\int_0^1\bigl(3+(1-t)^4\bigr)^3\,dt\\
&=\frac1{64}\left(27+\frac{27}{5}+1+\frac1{13}\right)\\
&=\frac{34}{65}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Therefore
\[
\Pr(F_z)=\frac{31}{65}.
\tag{13}
\]

If \(F_z\) occurs, then \(z\notin I_R\): it is adjacent to a local minimum, and every local minimum belongs to \(I_R\).

For a neighbor \(u\) of \(v\), call its branch **successful** if \(F_z\) occurs for each of the three children \(z\) of \(u\). The success probability is
\[
a=\left(\frac{31}{65}\right)^3.
\tag{14}
\]

The twelve events \(F_z\), for the twelve distance-2 vertices, use disjoint sets of priorities. They are therefore independent. They also use no priority in \(N[v]\), so they are independent of \(M_v\). Consequently, the four branch-success events are independent, each with probability \(a\).

On \(M_v\), every successful branch supplies a private neighbor \(u\) of \(v\): its other three neighbors are outside \(I_R\). Thus, if at least two branches are successful, then \(v\in C\). We have proved
\[
\Pr(v\in C)\ge q_0,
\qquad
q_0:=\frac15\left(6a^2-8a^3+3a^4\right).
\tag{15}
\]

For the constants below, it suffices that
\[
q_0>\frac3{250}.
\tag{16}
\]
To check this without numerical approximation, note that
\[
a=\frac{29791}{274625}>\frac{27}{250}.
\]
The probability that a \(\operatorname{Bin}(4,a)\) random variable is at least 2 is increasing in \(a\), and direct substitution gives
\[
\frac15\left(
6\left(\frac{27}{250}\right)^2
-8\left(\frac{27}{250}\right)^3
+3\left(\frac{27}{250}\right)^4
\right)
>\frac3{250}.
\]

## 5. Combining the estimates

Let \(G\) be 4-regular with girth at least \(34\), and put \(R=12\). Construct \(I_R\), then apply the improvement rule to obtain \(I^+\).

Every radius-16 ball in \(G\) is a tree: a non-tree edge in such a ball would produce a cycle of length at most \(33\). Since \(G\) is 4-regular, all these rooted balls are isomorphic.

The entire rule producing \(I^+\) has radius
\[
R+4=16
\]
and uses only independent identically distributed marks and comparisons. It is invariant under rooted graph isomorphisms. Therefore there is a common number \(p^+\) such that
\[
\Pr(v\in I^+)=p^+
\qquad\text{for every }v\in V(G).
\tag{17}
\]

This uniformity is important: a lower bound on \(\mathbb E|I^+|\) alone would not establish the claimed fractional-coloring bound.

Writing \(n=|V(G)|\), equations (5), (11), and (15) give
\[
np^+
=\mathbb E|I^+|
\ge
n\left(\frac13-\delta_{12}+\frac{q_0}{7}\right).
\]
Now
\[
\delta_{12}
=\frac{4\cdot3^{11}}{13!}
=\frac{708588}{6227020800}
<\frac1{8750},
\]
and \(q_0>3/250\). Consequently,
\[
\begin{aligned}
p^+
&>
\frac13-\frac1{8750}+\frac{3}{1750}\\
&=\frac13+\frac1{625}\\
&=\frac{628}{1875}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{18}
\]

Let \(\mu(A)=\Pr(I^+=A)\). Assign each independent set \(A\) the fractional-coloring weight
\[
\lambda_A=\frac{\mu(A)}{628/1875}.
\]
For every vertex \(v\),
\[
\sum_{A\ni v}\lambda_A
=
\frac{\Pr(v\in I^+)}{628/1875}
\ge1,
\]
while
\[
\sum_A\lambda_A=\frac{1875}{628}.
\]
This proves the theorem. ∎

## 6. What remains missing for the original problem

The result is compatible with either answer to the original question:

- perhaps every sufficiently high-girth 4-regular graph is 3-colorable;
- perhaps there are 4-chromatic examples of arbitrarily large girth, all having \(\chi_f<3\).

An ordinary 3-coloring is equivalent to finding an independent set \(A\) such that \(G-A\) is bipartite. The construction above produces a distribution of comparatively large independent sets, but gives **no guarantee that any of their complements is bipartite**. That is the unresolved step; fractional coverage cannot simply be rounded to three color classes.

Thus the definite conclusion is the explicit fractional bound
\[
\chi_f(G)\le \frac{1875}{628}<3
\qquad
\text{for 4-regular }G\text{ of girth at least }34.
\]
No ordinary 3-colorability theorem, and no family answering the requested existence question, has been established here. I make no novelty claim for this auxiliary result or claim to have independently verified the current literature status of the original problem.
