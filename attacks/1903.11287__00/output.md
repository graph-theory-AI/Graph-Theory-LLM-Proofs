# 1. Statement and notation

I use the following description of the graphs from the source construction. Up to exchanging the bipartition or complementing all subsets, this is the unique description consistent with the recursive construction and the stated edge count.

Let
\[
A_k=\{a_S:S\subseteq [k]\},\qquad
B_k=\{b_T:T\subseteq [k]\}.
\]
The edges of \(G_k\) are
\[
a_Sb_T\in E(G_k)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
T=S\ \text{ or }\ T=S\cup\{i\}\text{ for some }i\notin S.
\]
Thus \(G_k\) has \(2^{k+1}\) vertices and
\[
2^k+k2^{k-1}=(k+2)2^{k-1}
\]
edges.

A convex unit-distance realization means an injective map
\[
p:V(G_k)\longrightarrow \mathbb R^2
\]
whose image is in strictly convex position and for which
\[
\|p(u)-p(v)\|=1\qquad(uv\in E(G_k)).
\]
No condition on nonedges is needed below.

The results proved here are:

1. Every \(G_k\) has an ordinary, indeed generically faithful, unit-distance realization in the plane.
2. That natural realization is a planar projection of a cube and has at most \(2(k+1)\) extreme vertices, so it is not convex for \(k\ge2\).
3. \(G_1\) and \(G_2\) nevertheless have convex unit-distance realizations.
4. In any strictly convex realization, a fixed oriented edge vector can occur at most twice.
5. Consequently, for \(k\ge3\), almost all of the canonical \(6\)-cycles of \(G_k\) must be non-centrally symmetric. In particular, no realization in which all canonical \(6\)-cycles are centrally symmetric can be convex.

The last condition excludes the evident additive and recursively translational constructions, but it does not settle \(k\ge3\).

# 2. An ordinary unit-distance realization for every \(k\)

Identify \(\mathbb R^2\) with \(\mathbb C\). Choose unit complex numbers
\[
u_0,u_1,\dots,u_k\in S^1
\]
and define
\[
p(a_S)=\sum_{i\in S}(u_i-u_0),\qquad
p(b_S)=p(a_S)+u_0.
\]
Then
\[
p(b_S)-p(a_S)=u_0,
\]
and, if \(i\notin S\),
\[
p(b_{S\cup\{i\}})-p(a_S)
=(u_i-u_0)+u_0=u_i.
\]
Every edge therefore has unit length.

For a generic choice of the \(u_i\), all vertices are distinct and no nonedge has unit length. Indeed, every difference of two represented vertices is an integer linear combination
\[
L(u_0,\dots,u_k)=\sum_{j=0}^k c_j u_j.
\]
The identity \(|L|=1\) on the whole torus \((S^1)^{k+1}\) can hold only when exactly one \(c_j\) is nonzero and equals \(1\) or \(-1\): expand \(|L|^2-1\) into its Fourier coefficients. For cross-part differences, these exceptional cases are precisely \(T=S\) and \(T=S\cup\{i\}\). The finitely many other collision or unwanted-distance equations are proper real-analytic subsets of the parameter torus.

## Why this construction is not convex

Put
\[
g_0=u_0,\qquad g_i=u_i-u_0\quad(1\le i\le k).
\]
The represented point set is exactly
\[
\left\{\sum_{i=0}^k\varepsilon_i g_i:\varepsilon_i\in\{0,1\}\right\}.
\]
Its convex hull is therefore the zonotope
\[
Z=\sum_{i=0}^k[0,g_i].
\]
A planar zonotope generated by \(k+1\) segments has at most \(2(k+1)\) vertices. Hence, for \(k\ge2\),
\[
2^{k+1}>2(k+1),
\]
so most of the represented vertices are not extreme.

Thus the obvious unit-distance construction cannot answer the convex question.

# 3. Explicit convex realizations of \(G_1\) and \(G_2\)

The graph \(G_2\) is a \(6\)-cycle with one leaf attached at each of two opposite cycle vertices:
\[
a_\varnothing-b_1-a_1-b_{12}-a_2-b_2-a_\varnothing,
\]
with leaves \(b_\varnothing\) at \(a_\varnothing\) and \(a_{12}\) at \(b_{12}\).

Write
\[
u_\theta=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),
\]
where angles are in degrees. Define
\[
\begin{aligned}
x_0&=(0,0),\\
x_1&=x_0+u_0,\\
x_2&=x_1+u_{100},\\
x_3&=x_2+u_{150},\\
x_4&=x_3+u_{180},\\
x_5&=x_4+u_{280}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Since the six directions
\[
0,100,150,180,280,330
\]
come in opposite pairs,
\[
x_5+u_{330}=x_0.
\]
Now put
\[
y_0=x_0+u_{345},\qquad
y_3=x_3+u_{165}.
\]

Consider the cyclic sequence
\[
x_0,\ y_0,\ x_1,\ x_2,\ x_3,\ y_3,\ x_4,\ x_5.
\]
The successive side directions, written in one increasing lift to \(\mathbb R\), are
\[
345,\ 442.5,\ 460,\ 510,\ 525,\ 622.5,\ 640,\ 690.
\]
The final turn back to the first direction is from \(690\) to \(705\). All turns are strictly between \(0\) and \(180\) degrees. Equivalently, the nonzero side vectors occur in strict cyclic order and sum to zero. Hence these eight points are the vertices of a strictly convex octagon in the displayed order.

Assign
\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{graph vertex}&\text{point}\\ \hline
a_\varnothing&x_0\\
b_1&x_1\\
a_1&x_2\\
b_{12}&x_3\\
a_2&x_4\\
b_2&x_5\\
b_\varnothing&y_0\\
a_{12}&y_3.
\end{array}
\]
All six consecutive \(x_i x_{i+1}\) cycle edges have length \(1\), as do \(x_0y_0\) and \(x_3y_3\). This realizes \(G_2\).

Restricting to
\[
a_\varnothing,\ b_\varnothing,\ b_1,\ a_1
\]
gives a convex realization of \(G_1\cong P_4\).

Thus the first unresolved member after these arguments is \(G_3\).

# 4. A repeated-vector obstruction in strictly convex sets

The following elementary lemma is useful.

## Lemma 4.1

Let \(X\subset\mathbb R^2\) be in strictly convex position. Suppose that
\[
q_i-p_i=d,\qquad 1\le i\le r,
\]
where the \(2r\) points \(p_i,q_i\) are distinct and \(d\ne0\). Then \(r\le2\).

### Proof

Put \(P=\{p_1,\dots,p_r\}\) and \(K=\operatorname{conv}P\). Since every subset of a strictly convex set is itself in convex position, \(K\) is an \(r\)-gon. Moreover,
\[
\operatorname{conv}\bigl(P\cup(P+d)\bigr)=K+[0,d].
\]
The Minkowski sum of an \(r\)-gon and a segment has at most \(r+2\) vertices. If all \(2r\) displayed points were extreme, we would have
\[
2r\le r+2,
\]
hence \(r\le2\). \(\square\)

Orient every edge of \(G_k\) from \(A_k\) to \(B_k\). Distinct edges having the same oriented displacement vector have disjoint endpoints, because the realization is injective and two such edges cannot share a tail or a head. Lemma 4.1 therefore gives:

## Corollary 4.2

In a convex realization of \(G_k\), every oriented edge vector occurs at most twice. In particular, the realization uses at least
\[
\left\lceil\frac{|E(G_k)|}{2}\right\rceil
=
\left\lceil (k+2)2^{k-2}\right\rceil
\]
distinct oriented unit vectors.

For comparison, the additive construction in Section 2 uses only \(k+1\) vectors \(u_0,\dots,u_k\).

# 5. Canonical \(6\)-cycles must mostly be non-central

For \(S\subseteq[k]\) and distinct \(i,j\notin S\), there is an elementary \(6\)-cycle
\[
C(S;i,j):
\quad
a_S,\ b_{S i},\ a_{S i},\ b_{Sij},\ a_{S j},\ b_{S j},
\]
where \(Si\) abbreviates \(S\cup\{i\}\).

There are
\[
N_6(k)=\binom{k}{2}2^{k-2}
\]
such cycles.

Call this cycle centrally symmetric if its six represented points, in the displayed graph-cycle order, satisfy
\[
z_1+z_4=z_2+z_5=z_3+z_6.
\]
No assertion is made here that the graph-cycle order is the cyclic hull order.

For each \(T\subseteq[k]\), define the diagonal-edge vector
\[
d_T=p(b_T)-p(a_T).
\]

## Lemma 5.1

If \(C(S;i,j)\) is centrally symmetric, then
\[
d_{S i}=d_{S j}.
\]

### Proof

In the cycle order, the second side vector is
\[
p(a_{Si})-p(b_{Si})=-d_{Si},
\]
while the opposite, fifth side vector is
\[
p(b_{Sj})-p(a_{Sj})=d_{Sj}.
\]
Opposite side vectors of a centrally symmetric hexagon are negatives of one another. Hence
\[
d_{Sj}=-(-d_{Si})=d_{Si}.
\]
\(\square\)

By Lemma 4.1, no value of \(d_T\) can occur for three distinct \(T\)'s. At a fixed rank \(r\), there are \(\binom{k}{r}\) vectors \(d_T\), so they can support at most
\[
\left\lfloor\frac{\binom{k}{r}}2\right\rfloor
\]
equal pairs. Moreover, the pair
\[
\{Si,Sj\}
\]
determines \(S,i,j\), so two different elementary cycles cannot produce the same equality pair.

Therefore:

## Proposition 5.2

The number of centrally symmetric elementary \(6\)-cycles in a convex realization of \(G_k\) is at most
\[
\sum_{r=0}^k
\left\lfloor\frac{\binom{k}{r}}2\right\rfloor.
\]
If \(s_2(k)\) denotes the number of \(1\)'s in the binary expansion of \(k\), this equals
\[
\frac{2^k-2^{s_2(k)}}2.
\]

Indeed, over \(\mathbb F_2\),
\[
(1+x)^k=\prod_{j:\,k_j=1}(1+x^{2^j}),
\]
so exactly \(2^{s_2(k)}\) binomial coefficients \(\binom{k}{r}\) are odd.

Consequently, at least
\[
\boxed{
\binom{k}{2}2^{k-2}
-\frac{2^k-2^{s_2(k)}}2
}
\]
elementary \(6\)-cycles must be non-centrally symmetric.

For example:

- in \(G_3\), at least \(4\) of its \(6\) elementary cycles must be non-central;
- in \(G_4\), at least \(17\) of its \(24\) elementary cycles must be non-central.

In particular:

## Corollary 5.3

For every \(k\ge3\), there is no convex realization of \(G_k\) in which all elementary \(6\)-cycles are centrally symmetric.

This rules out the natural translation realization and any recursive ansatz that enforces pairwise equality of opposite edge vectors on every elementary cell.

A simpler local consequence is that among the base cycles \(C(\varnothing;i,j)\), the centrally symmetric ones form a matching on the coordinate set \([k]\). Indeed, two such cycles sharing an index would force three of
\[
d_{\{1\}},\dots,d_{\{k\}}
\]
to be equal, contradicting Lemma 4.1.

# 6. Why this does not settle the conjecture

Central symmetry of an equilateral \(6\)-cycle is not forced by convexity. For instance, take successive unit edge directions
\[
0^\circ,\ 30^\circ,\ 120^\circ,\ 150^\circ,\ 240^\circ,\ 270^\circ.
\]
Their sum is zero because
\[
u_0+u_{120}+u_{240}=0,\qquad
u_{30}+u_{150}+u_{270}=0.
\]
The directions occur in strict cyclic order, so their successive partial sums form a strictly convex equilateral hexagon. It is not centrally symmetric: the fourth edge direction is \(150^\circ\), rather than the \(180^\circ\) opposite of the first edge.

Thus Proposition 5.2 only says that a convex realization of \(G_k\) would have to use the genuinely flexible, non-central configurations on almost all elementary \(6\)-cycles. There is presently no contradiction between those local configurations on overlapping cycles.

The Maxwell count also becomes restrictive only at \(k=6\). If
\[
v=2^{k+1},\qquad e=(k+2)2^{k-1},
\]
then
\[
e-(2v-3)=(k-6)2^{k-1}+3.
\]
Hence every realization for \(k\ge6\) has an equilibrium-stress space of dimension at least this quantity. This alone is not an obstruction: the nonconvex realization from Section 2 exists for every \(k\).

# 7. A fully specified certified test for \(G_3\)

The remaining \(k=3\) case has \(16\) vertices and \(20\) edge equations. It can, in principle, be decided exactly by real quantifier elimination.

For each candidate cyclic order
\[
\pi=(v_0,\dots,v_{15}),
\]
introduce \(p_v=(x_v,y_v)\) and impose
\[
(x_u-x_v)^2+(y_u-y_v)^2=1
\qquad(uv\in E(G_3)).
\]
Strict convexity in the prescribed counterclockwise order is encoded by
\[
\det\!\bigl(
p_{\pi(t+1)}-p_{\pi(t)},
p_{\pi(s)}-p_{\pi(t)}
\bigr)>0
\]
for every \(t\) and every
\[
s\notin\{t,t+1\},
\]
with indices modulo \(16\). These inequalities say that every other point lies strictly to the left of every oriented hull side.

One may normalize
\[
p(a_\varnothing)=(0,0),\qquad
p(b_\varnothing)=(1,0),
\]
using translation and rotation. A certified negative result would require infeasibility certificates for all cyclic orders modulo reversal, rotation, and graph automorphisms. No such computation was carried out here; numerical optimization alone would not constitute a proof.

# Conclusion

The family is always a planar unit-distance family, and \(G_1,G_2\) are convexly realizable. For \(k\ge3\), however, every convex realization must be radically different from the additive construction: every oriented edge direction has multiplicity at most two, and almost every canonical \(6\)-cycle must be a non-centrally-symmetric equilateral hexagon. These restrictions do not yet decide \(G_3\), and therefore do not resolve the general open problem.
