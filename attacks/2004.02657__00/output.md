## 1. Statement of the partial result

Regard a 3-graph as the pure two-dimensional simplicial complex generated by its triples. A subdivision is always meant simplicially; thus every copy constructed below is a homeomorph in the sense of the conjecture.

For a finite two-dimensional complex \(K\), let
\[
\tau_\triangle(K)=\min\{|A|:A\subseteq V(K),\ A\cap \sigma\neq\varnothing
\text{ for every 2-face }\sigma\}.
\]
Define its topological triangle-transversal number by
\[
\tau_\triangle^{\mathrm{top}}(H)
 =\min\{\tau_\triangle(K): |K|\cong |H|,\ K\text{ a finite triangulation}\}.
\]

The main partial result is the following.

### Theorem 1
If \(\tau_\triangle^{\mathrm{top}}(H)\le 2\), then there is \(C_H>0\) such that every \(n\)-vertex 3-graph with at least
\[
C_H n^{5/2}
\]
edges contains a homeomorph of \(H\).

If \(\tau_\triangle^{\mathrm{top}}(H)\le 1\), the stronger bound \(C_Hn^2\) suffices.

In particular, the conjecture holds for every 3-graph whose facets all meet some fixed pair of vertices. This includes cones, books of triangles sharing an edge, suspensions of arbitrary graphs, and the sphere.

I also prove the following hierarchy.

### Theorem 2
For every fixed \(q\geq 1\),
\[
\tau_\triangle^{\mathrm{top}}(H)\le q
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\operatorname{ex}_{\mathrm{top}}(n,H)
 =O_H\!\left(n^{\,3-1/q}\right).
\]
The case \(q=2\) is Theorem 1.

The argument is elementary apart from the standard graph theorem that every fixed graph has linear topological Turán number.

## 2. Graph input

We use the following classical fixed-topological-minor theorem.

### Graph subdivision theorem
For every fixed finite graph \(F\), there is a constant \(\mu_F\) such that every graph \(X\) satisfying
\[
e(X)\geq \mu_F |V(X)|
\]
contains a subdivision of \(F\).

For example, one may embed \(F\) into a sufficiently large complete graph and apply the classical bounded-average-degree theorem for excluding a fixed complete-graph subdivision. No quantitative estimate on \(\mu_F\) is needed.

## 3. Intersections of two links

For a vertex \(a\in V(G)\), write \(L_G(a)\) for its link graph:
\[
xy\in E(L_G(a))\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad axy\in E(G).
\]
For distinct \(a,b\), define the common link
\[
J_{ab}=L_G(a)\cap L_G(b).
\]
Thus
\[
xy\in E(J_{ab})
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
axy,bxy\in E(G).
\]

For a pair \(xy\), write
\[
d(xy)=|\{z:xyz\in E(G)\}|.
\]
If \(m=e(G)\), then
\[
\sum_{xy}d(xy)=3m.
\]

There is an exact double-counting identity
\[
\sum_{\{a,b\}\in\binom{V(G)}2}e(J_{ab})
   =\sum_{\{x,y\}\in\binom{V(G)}2}\binom{d(xy)}2.       \tag{1}
\]
Indeed, both sides count pairs \((\{a,b\},\{x,y\})\) such that both \(axy\) and \(bxy\) are edges.

Put
\[
P=\binom n2,\qquad S=3m,\qquad \overline d=\frac SP.
\]
By Cauchy–Schwarz,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{xy}\binom{d(xy)}2
 &=\frac12\left(\sum_{xy}d(xy)^2-S\right)\\
 &\geq \frac12\left(\frac{S^2}{P}-S\right).
\end{aligned}
\]
Consequently, by (1), some pair \(a,b\) satisfies
\[
e(J_{ab})
 \geq \frac12\left(\overline d^{\,2}-\overline d\right).       \tag{2}
\]

Suppose now that
\[
m\geq Cn^{5/2}.
\]
Since \(P\leq n^2/2\),
\[
\overline d=\frac{3m}{P}\geq 6C\sqrt n.
\]
For \(C\geq1\) and \(n\geq3\), this is at least \(2\), and (2) gives
\[
e(J_{ab})\geq\frac{\overline d^{\,2}}4
             \geq 9C^2 n.                                  \tag{3}
\]

This is the source of the exponent \(5/2\): the second moment of the pair-codegrees produces a common link with linearly many edges.

## 4. Forcing suspensions

Let \(D_2=\{p,q\}\) be a two-point discrete complex, and let \(F\) be a graph. Its simplicial suspension is
\[
\Sigma F=D_2*F,
\]
the 3-graph with facets
\[
\{p,x,y\},\ \{q,x,y\}
\qquad(xy\in E(F)).
\]

### Proposition 3
For every fixed graph \(F\), there is \(C_F\) such that every \(n\)-vertex 3-graph with at least \(C_Fn^{5/2}\) edges contains a subdivision of \(\Sigma F\).

#### Proof
Choose \(C_F\geq1\) so that
\[
9C_F^2\geq \mu_F+1.
\]
By (3), there are distinct \(a,b\in V(G)\) for which
\[
e(J_{ab})\geq(\mu_F+1)n
           \geq \mu_F |V(J_{ab})|.
\]
The graph subdivision theorem gives a subdivision \(F'\) of \(F\) inside \(J_{ab}\).

For every edge \(xy\in E(F')\), the definition of \(J_{ab}\) gives
\[
axy,bxy\in E(G).
\]
These triples form the complex \(\{a,b\}*F'\).

If an edge \(uv\) of \(F\) is represented in \(F'\) by a path
\[
u=z_0,z_1,\ldots,z_k=v,
\]
then the original triangle \(auv\) is triangulated by
\[
az_{i-1}z_i,\qquad 1\leq i\leq k,
\]
and similarly on the \(b\)-side. Hence \(\{a,b\}*F'\) is a simplicial subdivision of \(\Sigma F\). ∎

Taking \(F\) to be a cycle recovers the \(O(n^{5/2})\) upper bound for a triangulated sphere.

## 5. Reduction from a two-vertex facet cover

Suppose \(K\) has a pair \(p,q\) meeting every facet. Some facets may contain both \(p\) and \(q\). Perform a stellar subdivision of the edge \(pq\): introduce a vertex \(z\), and replace every facet
\[
pqx
\]
by
\[
pzx,\qquad qzx.
\]
Leave all other facets unchanged. The resulting complex \(K'\) is a subdivision of \(K\), and every facet of \(K'\) contains exactly one of \(p,q\).

Let
\[
W=V(K')\setminus\{p,q\},
\]
and define a graph \(F\) on \(W\) by declaring \(xy\in E(F)\) whenever at least one of \(pxy,qxy\) is a facet of \(K'\). Then
\[
K'\subseteq \Sigma F.                                      \tag{4}
\]

Proposition 3 gives a subdivision of \(\Sigma F\) in \(G\). Restrict it to the facets corresponding to \(K'\) in (4). More explicitly, if \(xy\in E(F)\) is represented by a path \(P_{xy}\), then a facet \(pxy\) of \(K'\) is replaced by the fan from the first suspension vertex over \(P_{xy}\); similarly for \(qxy\). Since the paths representing distinct edges of \(F\) are internally disjoint, these fans form a compatible subdivision of \(K'\).

Thus \(G\) contains a subdivision of \(K'\), and hence a homeomorph of \(K\). Applying this to a triangulation \(K\cong H\) proves Theorem 1.

### The one-vertex case
If all facets of \(H\) contain a fixed vertex \(p\), then \(H=p*F\) for a graph \(F\). Since
\[
\sum_{v\in V(G)}e(L_G(v))=3e(G),
\]
a 3-graph with \(Cn^2\) edges has a link with at least \(3Cn\) edges. For \(C\geq\mu_F/3\), that link contains a subdivision of \(F\), and coning it from \(v\) gives a subdivision of \(H\).

## 6. General \(q\)-fold common-link bound

The same argument gives Theorem 2.

For \(A\in\binom{V(G)}q\), define
\[
J_A=\bigcap_{a\in A}L_G(a).
\]
Double counting gives
\[
\sum_{A\in\binom{V(G)}q}e(J_A)
   =\sum_{xy}\binom{d(xy)}q.                                \tag{5}
\]

The sequence \(k\mapsto\binom{k}{q}\) is discretely convex. Hence, if
\(\overline d=P^{-1}\sum d(xy)\) and \(\overline d\geq4q\), then
\[
\sum_{xy}\binom{d(xy)}q
 \geq P\binom{\lfloor\overline d\rfloor}{q}
 \geq \frac{P}{q!}\left(\frac{\overline d}{2}\right)^q.       \tag{6}
\]
If
\[
m\geq Cn^{3-1/q},
\]
then
\[
\overline d=\frac{3m}{P}\geq6C n^{1-1/q}.
\]
Using \(P\geq n^2/4\) and \(\binom nq\leq n^q/q!\), equations (5)–(6) imply that some \(A\) satisfies
\[
e(J_A)\geq
 \frac{P}{n^q}\left(\frac{\overline d}{2}\right)^q
 \geq \frac{3^q}{4}C^q n.                                  \tag{7}
\]
Taking \(C\) sufficiently large in terms of \(q\) and \(F\), the graph \(J_A\) contains a subdivision of \(F\). Therefore \(G\) contains a subdivision of
\[
D_q*F,
\]
where \(D_q\) is a discrete set of \(q\) suspension vertices.

It remains to connect this to a \(q\)-vertex facet cover. Let \(A\) meet every facet of \(H\). There is a subdivision \(H'\) in which every small triangle contains exactly one member of \(A\):

- A triangle containing one member of \(A\) is left unchanged.
- If it contains two members of \(A\), subdivide their common edge and split the triangle into two.
- If all three vertices lie in \(A\), subdivide its three boundary edges, add a face-centre, and divide the resulting boundary hexagon into six triangles around that centre.

These subdivisions agree along common edges. Defining \(F\) from the pairs opposite the vertices of \(A\), one obtains
\[
H'\subseteq D_q*F.
\]
Combining this with (7) proves
\[
\operatorname{ex}_{\mathrm{top}}(n,H)
 =O_H(n^{3-1/q}).
\]

Finite exceptional values of \(n\) are handled by enlarging the constant.

## 7. Why this does not prove the full conjecture

The two-vertex reduction has a genuine topological limitation. In fact:

### Observation
A connected closed triangulated surface with a two-vertex facet transversal is homeomorphic to \(S^2\).

To see this, apply the subdivision above so that every triangle contains exactly one of \(p,q\), and write the resulting complex as a subcomplex of \(\Sigma F\). For every used base edge \(xy\), closedness requires exactly two incident triangles. The only possibilities are \(pxy\) and \(qxy\), so both must occur. Thus the surface is \(\Sigma F_0\) for the graph \(F_0\) of used base edges.

The triangles incident with an edge \(px\) correspond to the edges of \(F_0\) incident with \(x\). Since every manifold edge belongs to exactly two triangles,
\[
\deg_{F_0}(x)=2.
\]
Moreover, the link of \(p\) must be a single cycle. Hence \(F_0\) is one cycle and
\[
\Sigma F_0\cong S^2.
\]

Consequently, the torus and every other closed surface besides the sphere have topological triangle-transversal number greater than two. The common-link argument above therefore does not settle even the torus case.

For \(q>2\), the direct moment method gives \(n^{3-1/q}\), which is strictly larger than \(n^{5/2}\). Reaching \(5/2\) for arbitrary \(H\) would require coordinating subdivisions lying in several different common-link graphs while preserving their shared boundary data. No such synchronization is provided by the averaging argument.

Thus the full conjecture remains open; the result above is a rigorous special case and a transversal-dependent hierarchy, not a general proof.
