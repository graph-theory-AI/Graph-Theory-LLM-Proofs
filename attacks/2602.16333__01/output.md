## 1. Statement and conventions

Write \(\operatorname{circ}(D)\) for the maximum number of vertices in a simple directed cycle of \(D\). I use the usual convention that a digraph may contain both \(xy\) and \(yx\). Loops are irrelevant and may be deleted.

The conjecture asks whether there is an absolute \(K>0\) such that every connected vertex-transitive digraph \(D\) on \(n\) vertices satisfies
\[
\operatorname{circ}(D)\ge Kc(n).
\]

The reverse inequality is immediate:

### Proposition 1
For every \(n\ge 3\),
\[
d(n)\le c(n).
\]

#### Proof
Given a connected vertex-transitive graph \(G\), replace each edge \(xy\) by the two arcs \(xy,yx\). The resulting symmetric digraph is connected and vertex-transitive, and its directed circumference equals the undirected circumference of \(G\). Taking minima proves the assertion. \(\square\)

Thus only \(d(n)=\Omega(c(n))\) is at issue.

A minor useful fact is that “connected” and “strongly connected” coincide here.

### Lemma 2
Every finite weakly connected vertex-transitive digraph is strongly connected.

#### Proof
Automorphisms permute the strongly connected components transitively. If the condensation had more than one vertex, choose a source component. By transitivity every component would be a source, so there could be no arcs between distinct components, contradicting weak connectivity. \(\square\)

## 2. A cyclic-extension stitching lemma

For a finite group \(G\) and \(S\subseteq G\), let
\[
\operatorname{Cay}(G,S)
\]
denote the digraph with arcs \(g\to gs\), \(s\in S\).

### Lemma 3
Let \(H\trianglelefteq G\), let \(s\in S\), and suppose
\[
G/H=\langle sH\rangle
\]
has order \(q=[G:H]\). Suppose \(T\subseteq S\cap H\) generates \(H\), and \(\operatorname{Cay}(H,T)\) has a directed Hamilton path. Then:

1. \(\operatorname{Cay}(G,S)\) has a directed Hamilton path;
2. \(\operatorname{Cay}(G,S)\) has a directed cycle of length at least
   \[
   (q-1)|H|+1=|G|-|H|+1.
   \]

#### Proof
Let
\[
P=(p_0=1,p_1,\ldots,p_{m-1}=h),\qquad m=|H|,
\]
be a Hamilton path in \(\operatorname{Cay}(H,T)\). Define
\[
x_0=1,\qquad x_{j+1}=x_jhs.
\]
Since \(H\trianglelefteq G\),
\[
x_{j+1}H=x_jhsH=x_jsH,
\]
and hence
\[
x_jH=s^jH.
\]
Thus the \(q\) left cosets \(x_jH\), \(0\le j<q\), are pairwise distinct.

Translate \(P\) into each coset:
\[
x_jp_0,x_jp_1,\ldots,x_jp_{m-1}.
\]
After reaching \(x_jh\), use the \(s\)-arc
\[
x_jh\longrightarrow x_jhs=x_{j+1}.
\]
Concatenating all \(q\) translated copies yields a Hamilton path in \(G\).

For the cycle, traverse only the first \(q-1\) copies, and then use the \(s\)-arc into
\[
z=x_{q-1}.
\]
Now
\[
zH=s^{q-1}H=s^{-1}H.
\]
Therefore \(z\) and \(s^{-1}\) lie in the same left coset of \(H\). Because \(T\) generates the finite group \(H\), positive \(T\)-words reach every element of \(H\); hence there is a simple directed \(T\)-path inside \(zH\) from \(z\) to \(s^{-1}\). Finally use
\[
s^{-1}\longrightarrow 1.
\]

The first \(q-1\) copies contain \((q-1)|H|\) vertices, and the last path contributes at least one vertex from the remaining coset. All these vertices are distinct because the cosets are distinct. This gives the claimed cycle length. \(\square\)

This also applies whenever the connection set contains a sequence \(s_1,\dots,s_k\) for which
\[
G_i=\langle s_1,\dots,s_i\rangle,\qquad G_{i-1}\trianglelefteq G_i.
\]
Repeated use of the path part constructs a Hamilton path in \(G_{k-1}\), after which the cycle part applies at the top level.

## 3. Abelian Cayley digraphs have linear circumference

### Theorem 4
Let \(A\) be a finite abelian group of order \(n\), and let \(S\subseteq A\) generate \(A\). Then
\[
\operatorname{circ}(\operatorname{Cay}(A,S))\ge \frac n2.
\]
More precisely, let \(S_0\subseteq S\) be an inclusion-minimal generating set. For any \(s\in S_0\), put
\[
H=\langle S_0\setminus\{s\}\rangle,\qquad q=[A:H].
\]
Then
\[
\operatorname{circ}(\operatorname{Cay}(A,S))
   \ge n-|H|+1
   =n-\frac nq+1.
\]

#### Proof
First observe that every connected Cayley digraph of a finite abelian group has a directed Hamilton path. Indeed, order a minimal generating set as \(s_1,\dots,s_k\), put
\[
A_i=\langle s_1,\dots,s_i\rangle,
\]
and start with the one-vertex path in \(A_0\). Since \(A_{i-1}\trianglelefteq A_i\) and \(A_i/A_{i-1}\) is cyclic, generated by \(s_i+A_{i-1}\), the path part of Lemma 3 inductively produces a Hamilton path in \(A_i\).

Now fix \(s\in S_0\) and take \(H=\langle S_0\setminus\{s\}\rangle\). The preceding argument gives a Hamilton path in the Cayley digraph on \(H\). Since \(A/H\) is cyclic and generated by \(s+H\), the cycle part of Lemma 3 gives
\[
\operatorname{circ}(\operatorname{Cay}(A,S))
   \ge n-|H|+1.
\]
Minimality of \(S_0\) gives \(q=[A:H]\ge2\), so \(|H|\le n/2\). \(\square\)

### Corollary 5
If a connected vertex-transitive digraph \(D\) on \(n\) vertices admits a regular abelian subgroup of automorphisms, then
\[
\operatorname{circ}(D)\ge \frac n2\ge \frac{c(n)}2.
\]

#### Proof
A digraph admitting a regular automorphism group \(A\) is a Cayley digraph on \(A\). Connectedness implies that its connection set generates \(A\). Apply Theorem 4 and use \(c(n)\le n\). \(\square\)

Thus no family of abelian Cayley digraphs—and, more generally, no family having a compatible cyclic normal generating chain as above—can disprove the conjecture.

For circulant digraphs this says in particular that every connected circulant has circumference at least \(n/2\). If its connection set contains a residue coprime to \(n\), that single step already gives a Hamilton cycle.

## 4. Exact resolution for prime orders

### Theorem 6
For every prime \(p\ge3\),
\[
d(p)=c(p)=p.
\]

#### Proof
Let \(D\) be a connected vertex-transitive digraph on \(p\) vertices, and let \(\Gamma\le\operatorname{Aut}(D)\) act transitively. By orbit–stabilizer, \(p\mid |\Gamma|\), so \(\Gamma\) contains an element \(\tau\) of order \(p\). As a nonidentity permutation of \(p\) points, \(\tau\) is a \(p\)-cycle.

Choose a non-loop arc \(v\to\tau^a(v)\), which exists by connectedness. Here \(a\not\equiv0\pmod p\). Applying powers of \(\tau\) shows that
\[
\tau^i(v)\longrightarrow \tau^{i+a}(v)
\]
is an arc for every \(i\). Since \(a\) generates \(\mathbb Z_p\), these arcs form a directed Hamilton cycle.

The same argument applied to an edge of an undirected connected vertex-transitive graph gives an undirected Hamilton cycle. Hence every relevant graph and digraph has circumference \(p\). \(\square\)

## 5. Further necessary conditions on a counterexample

### Degree bound
If \(D\) has constant non-loop outdegree \(r\), then
\[
\operatorname{circ}(D)\ge r+1.
\]

Indeed, let
\[
v_0\to v_1\to\cdots\to v_\ell
\]
be a longest directed path. Every outneighbor of \(v_\ell\) lies on the path. Among the \(r\) corresponding indices, the smallest is at most \(\ell-r\); if it is \(i\), then
\[
v_i\to v_{i+1}\to\cdots\to v_\ell\to v_i
\]
has length at least \(r+1\).

Consequently, if the conjecture is false and \(D_j\) is a sequence with
\[
\frac{\operatorname{circ}(D_j)}{c(|D_j|)}\longrightarrow0,
\]
then its outdegrees satisfy
\[
\frac{r(D_j)}{c(|D_j|)}\longrightarrow0.
\]
In particular, linearly dense vertex-transitive digraphs satisfy the desired comparison.

### Mutual arcs
Let \(M(D)\) be the undirected graph whose edges are the pairs \(xy\) for which both \(xy\) and \(yx\) are arcs of \(D\). This is an invariant vertex-transitive graph, though it may be disconnected.

If \(M(D)\) is connected, then
\[
\operatorname{circ}(D)\ge c(n),
\]
because every cycle of \(M(D)\) can be directed consistently in \(D\).

More generally, every component \(B\) of \(M(D)\) is vertex-transitive under its setwise stabilizer. Hence, for \(|B|\ge3\),
\[
\operatorname{circ}(D)\ge c(|B|).
\]
Thus a counterexample family must also have disconnected mutual-arc graphs.

Combining these observations, any genuine obstruction must eventually be sparse relative to \(c(n)\), have disconnected symmetric part, and admit no regular abelian automorphism group.

## 6. Why the underlying undirected graph does not immediately solve the problem

For every \(D\), its symmetrization \(U(D)\), obtained by forgetting arc directions, is a connected vertex-transitive graph and therefore has an undirected cycle of length at least \(c(n)\). The missing step is to turn such a cycle into a comparably long directed cycle.

Strong connectivity alone is far from sufficient. For example, let
\[
V=\{h\}\cup\{a_i,b_i:i\in\mathbb Z_m\},
\]
and include:

- both arcs between \(h\) and every \(a_i,b_i\);
- arcs \(a_i\to b_i\) and \(a_i\to b_{i-1}\).

This digraph is strongly connected. Its underlying graph contains the \(2m\)-cycle
\[
a_0,b_0,a_1,b_1,\ldots,a_{m-1},b_{m-1},a_0.
\]
Nevertheless every directed cycle has length at most \(3\): after deleting \(h\), every arc goes from an \(a\)-vertex to a \(b\)-vertex, while each \(b\)-vertex can only return to \(h\). This example is not regular or vertex-transitive, but it shows precisely why replacing backwards edges by short directed return paths can collapse through a common bottleneck.

The abelian Cayley proof avoids this collapse by arranging translated Hamilton paths in disjoint group cosets. A general vertex-transitive digraph need not possess a regular subgroup at all; it is naturally a coset digraph rather than a Cayley digraph. Even for nonabelian Cayley digraphs, a minimal connection set need not generate a normal subgroup chain, so the coset-stitching argument above does not apply.

That is the unresolved gap.

## 7. Conclusion and bibliographic caveat

The conjecture is not proved. The concrete progress is:

\[
\boxed{\operatorname{circ}(D)\ge \tfrac12 c(n)}
\]
for every connected vertex-transitive \(D\) admitting a regular abelian automorphism group, with the stronger bound
\[
\operatorname{circ}(D)\ge n-\frac nq+1
\]
from a minimal generating set; and
\[
\boxed{d(p)=c(p)=p}
\]
for every prime \(p\).

These results do not improve the universal \(\Omega(n^{1/3})\) bound stated in the question, because the minimum defining \(d(n)\) ranges over non-Cayley and nonabelian examples as well. I have not checked whether the elementary abelian Cayley lemma already appears in the literature.

Finally, the supplied literature summary contains a numerical inconsistency:
\[
\frac{13}{21}=\frac{26}{42}<\frac{27}{42}=\frac9{14}.
\]
Thus an \(\Omega(n^{13/21})\) lower bound is not, as a pure exponent statement, an improvement over \(\Omega(n^{9/14})\). None of the arguments above uses either exponent.
