## Statement and interpretation

Let \(R\) be a finite ring with \(1\neq0\), and let
\[
X_R=\operatorname{Cay}(R^+,R^\times),
\]
so that \(V(X_R)=R\) and distinct \(x,y\) are adjacent exactly when \(x-y\in R^\times\).

A set \(M\subseteq V(G)\) is a module (homogeneous set) if every vertex outside \(M\) is either complete or anticomplete to \(M\). A graph is prime if it has no module \(M\) with
\[
1<|M|<|V(G)|.
\]

For a semisimple finite ring, write its Wedderburn decomposition as
\[
R\cong\prod_{i=1}^t M_{n_i}(\mathbb F_{q_i}),
\]
and put
\[
k_2(R)=\bigl|\{i:n_i=1,\ q_i=2\}\bigr|,
\]
counting the direct factors isomorphic to \(\mathbb F_2\).

### Classification

For \(|R|\ge3\),
\[
\boxed{
X_R\text{ is prime}
\iff
\begin{cases}
J(R)=0,\\
R\text{ is not a field},\\
k_2(R)\le1.
\end{cases}}
\]

Equivalently, \(R\) must be semisimple, its Wedderburn decomposition must contain either at least two simple factors or at least one matrix factor \(M_n(\mathbb F_q)\) with \(n\ge2\), and it may contain at most one factor \(\mathbb F_2\).

Under the convention that a graph with two vertices is prime whenever it has no proper nontrivial module, \(R=\mathbb F_2\) is one additional case because \(X_{\mathbb F_2}=K_2\). If “prime” is required to have at least four vertices, this exception is omitted.

This is the content of Theorem 4.35 of the supplied source paper. The proof below is included to verify the classification independently.

---

## 1. The Jacobson-radical obstruction

Let \(J=J(R)\). Recall that
\[
a\in R^\times \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a+J\in(R/J)^\times.
\]

Indeed, one implication is immediate. Conversely, if \(a+J\) is invertible, then \(ab=1+j\) for some \(b\in R\) and \(j\in J\); since \(1+j\) is a unit, \(a\) has a right inverse, and in a finite ring this implies that \(a\) is a unit.

Consequently, every coset \(C=c+J\) is a module of \(X_R\). If \(a,b\in C\) and \(z\notin C\), then
\[
(z-a)+J=(z-b)+J,
\]
and hence
\[
z-a\in R^\times \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z-b\in R^\times.
\]
Thus \(z\) is adjacent either to all of \(C\) or to none of \(C\).

If \(J\neq0\), then \(|C|=|J|>1\), while \(C\neq R\) because \(1\notin J\). Therefore \(C\) is a proper nontrivial module. Hence
\[
X_R\text{ prime}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad J(R)=0.
\]

In fact,
\[
X_R\cong X_{R/J}\bigl[\overline{K}_{|J|}\bigr],
\]
a uniform independent-set blow-up of \(X_{R/J}\).

---

## 2. Connectivity of \(X_R\) in the semisimple case

For any Cayley graph \(\operatorname{Cay}(A,S)\), the component of \(0\) is the subgroup generated by \(S\). Thus \(X_R\) is connected exactly when \(R^\times\) generates \(R^+\).

Let
\[
R=\prod_{i=1}^t A_i,\qquad
A_i=M_{n_i}(\mathbb F_{q_i}).
\]

For a simple factor \(A\), define
\[
\Delta(A)=\langle u-v:u,v\in A^\times\rangle_+.
\]

We claim that
\[
\Delta(A)=
\begin{cases}
0,&A=\mathbb F_2,\\
A,&A\not\cong\mathbb F_2.
\end{cases}
\]

For a field \(\mathbb F_q\) with \(q>2\), the difference of two distinct nonzero elements is nonzero, and multiplication by units shows that all of \(\mathbb F_q\) lies in \(\Delta(\mathbb F_q)\).

For \(A=M_n(\mathbb F_q)\), \(n\ge2\), first note that invertible matrices additively generate \(A\). For \(i\neq j\),
\[
\lambda E_{ij}=(I+\lambda E_{ij})-I,
\]
and both matrices on the right are invertible. For a diagonal matrix unit, on the \(i,j\) coordinates use
\[
P=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
Q=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
P+Q=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
After adjoining complementary blocks \(I\) and \(-I\), this expresses every scalar multiple of \(E_{ii}\) as a sum of two invertible matrices.

It remains to see that \(\Delta(A)\) contains a unit. If \(q>2\), choose \(\lambda\notin\{0,1\}\); then \(I,\lambda I\), and \(I-\lambda I\) are all invertible. If \(q=2\) and \(n\ge2\), identify \(\mathbb F_2^n\) with \(\mathbb F_{2^n}\), choose \(\alpha\notin\{0,1\}\), and let \(T\) be multiplication by \(\alpha\). Both \(T\) and \(I-T\) are invertible. Thus in every matrix case \(\Delta(A)\) contains a unit. Since it is invariant under left multiplication by units, it contains every unit, and hence all of \(A\).

Now let \(B\) be the set of indices for which \(A_i=\mathbb F_2\). Differences of global units which agree in every coordinate except \(i\) show that the additive subgroup generated by \(R^\times\) contains the entire \(i\)-th coordinate subgroup whenever \(i\notin B\). On the other hand, every global unit has coordinate \(1\) in every \(\mathbb F_2\)-factor. It follows that
\[
\langle R^\times\rangle_+
=
\left\{x\in R:x_i=x_j\text{ for every }i,j\in B\right\}.
\]

Therefore
\[
X_R\text{ is connected}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k_2(R)\le1.
\]

If \(k_2(R)=k\ge2\), the graph has exactly \(2^{k-1}\) connected components.

## 3. Connectivity of the complement

The complement \(\overline{X_R}\) is the Cayley graph whose connection set consists of the nonzero nonunits of \(R\). Thus it is connected exactly when the nonzero nonunits generate \(R^+\).

For semisimple \(R\):

- If \(R\) is a field, there are no nonzero nonunits, so \(\overline{X_R}\) is edgeless.
- If \(R=M_n(\mathbb F_q)\) with \(n\ge2\), all matrices \(\lambda E_{ij}\), \(\lambda\neq0\), are nonzero singular matrices, and they generate the additive group.
- If \(R\) has at least two simple factors, every nonzero element supported on a single factor is a nonunit because all other coordinates are zero. Such elements generate every coordinate subgroup.

Hence
\[
\overline{X_R}\text{ is connected}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
R\text{ is not a field}.
\]

---

## 4. From a module to a two-sided ideal

We use the following standard modular-decomposition fact.

**Lemma.** Let \(G\) be a finite connected graph whose complement is connected. If \(G\) is not prime, then its maximal proper strong modules form a canonical partition with at least one nonsingleton part. This partition is invariant under every automorphism of \(G\).

Here a module is strong if it does not overlap any other module. The lemma follows from the elementary modular-decomposition trichotomy: after contracting maximal proper strong modules, the quotient is complete, edgeless, or prime. The first two alternatives would make respectively \(\overline G\) or \(G\) disconnected. If all parts were singletons, the quotient would be \(G\) itself and would be prime.

Apply this to \(G=X_R\), assuming that both \(X_R\) and its complement are connected. Let \(H\) be the canonical part containing \(0\).

Every additive translation
\[
\tau_a:x\longmapsto x+a
\]
is an automorphism of \(X_R\). For \(h\in H\), the part \(\tau_h(H)\) contains \(h\), so it must equal \(H\). Thus \(H+h=H\) for every \(h\in H\), and \(H\) is an additive subgroup. The parts are precisely the additive cosets of \(H\).

For each unit \(u\in R^\times\), both maps
\[
L_u:x\longmapsto ux,\qquad R_u:x\longmapsto xu
\]
are graph automorphisms fixing \(0\). By canonicity they preserve \(H\). Since connectedness of \(X_R\) says that the units additively generate \(R\), it follows that for all \(r\in R\),
\[
rH\subseteq H,\qquad Hr\subseteq H.
\]
Therefore \(H\) is a nonzero proper two-sided ideal of \(R\).

---

## 5. Semisimplicity rules out such a module

Assume now that \(R\) is semisimple. Every two-sided ideal \(H\) is a direct summand and has an identity element \(e\in H\). If \(H\) is nonzero and proper, then \(e\neq0,1\).

The vertex \(1\) lies outside \(H\). It is adjacent to \(0\in H\), since \(1\) is a unit. But it is not adjacent to \(e\), since
\[
1-e
\]
is not a unit: if it were, then from \(e(1-e)=0\) one would obtain \(e=0\). Thus the outside vertex \(1\) distinguishes \(0\) from \(e\), contradicting the assertion that \(H\) is a module.

Consequently:

> If \(R\) is semisimple and both \(X_R\) and \(\overline{X_R}\) are connected, then \(X_R\) is prime.

Combining this with Sections 2 and 3 gives the sufficiency of the stated conditions.

---

## 6. Necessity and completion of the classification

The three obstructions are exhaustive:

1. **\(J(R)\neq0\):** every Jacobson-radical coset is a proper nontrivial module.
2. **\(R\) is a field of order at least three:** \(X_R=K_{|R|}\), and any two vertices form a module.
3. **At least two \(\mathbb F_2\)-factors:** \(X_R\) is disconnected. Each connected component has at least two vertices, since \(x\) is adjacent to \(x+1\), and hence a component is a proper nontrivial module.

Conversely, if none of these occurs, then \(R\) is semisimple, \(X_R\) is connected, and its complement is connected. Section 5 then proves that \(X_R\) is prime.

This completes the classification.

## 7. Tensor products of complete graphs

For a product of fields
\[
R=\prod_{i=1}^t\mathbb F_{q_i},
\]
one has
\[
X_R=K_{q_1}\times\cdots\times K_{q_t},
\]
where \(\times\) is the categorical/tensor graph product: two tuples are adjacent exactly when they differ in every coordinate.

Therefore, for \(t\ge2\),
\[
K_{q_1}\times\cdots\times K_{q_t}
\text{ is prime}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\bigl|\{i:q_i=2\}\bigr|\le1.
\]
For example,
\[
K_2\times K_2=2K_2
\]
is not prime, whereas
\[
K_2\times K_3\cong C_6
\]
is prime.

## Status and gaps

There is no remaining mathematical gap under the standard definitions above. Question 4.28 is an organizing question answered later in the same paper by Theorem 4.35; the catalog’s suggestion that the arbitrary finite-ring case remains open appears to conflate \(X_R\) with later \(p\)-unitary variants. The only genuine ambiguity in the extracted statement is the convention for the two-vertex graph \(X_{\mathbb F_2}=K_2\).
