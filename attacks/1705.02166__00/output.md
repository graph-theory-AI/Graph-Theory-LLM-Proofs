## 1. Formulation and partial results

Write \(R\) for the red set and \(B=\mathbb R^n\setminus R\) for the blue set. I interpret an isometric copy as the image under an ambient Euclidean isometry.

A coloring avoids both alternatives precisely when

1. \(R\) contains no pair at distance one; and
2. \(R\) intersects every isometric copy of \(K\).

Thus
\[
\mathbb R^n\nrightarrow(\ell_2,K)
\]
if and only if there is a unit-distance-independent set \(R\) which is a transversal of all congruent copies of \(K\).

The following partial classification will be proved.

### Theorem A: a universal finite-cardinality range

For every \(n\ge 2\) and every nonempty finite \(K\subset\mathbb R^n\),
\[
|K|\le n+1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathbb R^n\to(\ell_2,K).
\]

In particular, every three-point configuration in the plane is forced.

### Theorem B: complete classification for two-point sets

Let \(K\) consist of two points at distance \(r>0\).

- If \(n\ge2\), then
  \[
  \mathbb R^n\to(\ell_2,K)
  \]
  for every \(r\).

- In dimension one, write \(r=p/q\) in lowest terms when \(r\) is rational. Then
  \[
  \mathbb R\to(\ell_2,K)
  \]
  if and only if \(p\) and \(q\) have opposite parity.

Thus irrational \(r\), and rational \(r=p/q\) with \(p,q\) both odd, admit avoiding colorings.

### Theorem C: a broad negative class

If
\[
\operatorname{int}(\overline K)\ne\varnothing,
\]
then for every \(n\ge1\),
\[
\mathbb R^n\nrightarrow(\ell_2,K).
\]

The countercoloring can be chosen with the red set open and relatively dense.

I also give an exact finite-state decision procedure for finite one-dimensional configurations whose pairwise differences are rational, and a compactness reduction for arbitrary finite \(K\).

## 2. A finite-shift averaging lemma

The arguments must accommodate completely arbitrary, possibly nonmeasurable colorings.

### Lemma 2.1

Let \(P,Q\subset\mathbb R^n\) be finite nonempty sets, and let \(f:\mathbb R^n\to[0,1]\). Suppose that for every \(x\in\mathbb R^n\),
\[
\sum_{p\in P}f(x+p)\ge a
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\sum_{q\in Q}f(x+q)\le b.
\]
Then
\[
\frac{a}{|P|}\le \frac{b}{|Q|}.
\]

#### Proof

Let \(\Gamma\) be the subgroup of \((\mathbb R^n,+)\) generated by \(P\cup Q\). It is a finitely generated torsion-free abelian group, hence isomorphic to \(\mathbb Z^d\) for some \(d\). Choose a basis and let \(F_N\) be the corresponding boxes \([-N,N]^d\).

For every fixed \(t\in\Gamma\),
\[
\frac{|(F_N+t)\mathbin{\triangle}F_N|}{|F_N|}\longrightarrow0.
\]
After passing to a subsequence, assume
\[
\frac1{|F_N|}\sum_{x\in F_N}f(x)\longrightarrow\delta.
\]
The Følner property then gives, for every fixed \(t\in P\cup Q\),
\[
\frac1{|F_N|}\sum_{x\in F_N}f(x+t)\longrightarrow\delta.
\]
Averaging the two assumed inequalities and taking limits gives
\[
|P|\delta\ge a,\qquad |Q|\delta\le b.
\]
The result follows. \(\square\)

### Corollary 2.2

Let \(H\) be a finite graph drawn in \(\mathbb R^n\) so that every edge has Euclidean length one. If \(v(H)=v\) and \(\alpha(H)=a\), then every finite \(m\)-point set \(K\subset\mathbb R^n\) satisfying
\[
\frac{v}{a}>m
\]
obeys
\[
\mathbb R^n\to(\ell_2,K).
\]

#### Proof

Suppose an avoiding coloring exists, and let \(f=1_R\).

Since no translated copy \(x+K\) can be all blue,
\[
\sum_{k\in K}f(x+k)\ge1
\]
for every \(x\).

On the other hand, the red vertices in every translate \(x+V(H)\) form an independent set of \(H\), so
\[
\sum_{v\in V(H)}f(x+v)\le \alpha(H).
\]
Lemma 2.1 yields
\[
\frac1m\le\frac{\alpha(H)}{v(H)},
\]
contrary to \(v(H)/\alpha(H)>m\). \(\square\)

## 3. A generalized spindle and proof of Theorem A

### Lemma 3.1

For every \(n\ge2\), there is a finite graph \(H_n\) drawn in \(\mathbb R^n\), with every edge of length one, such that
\[
|V(H_n)|=2n+3,\qquad \alpha(H_n)=2.
\]

#### Construction

Set
\[
D=\sqrt{\frac{2(n+1)}n},
\qquad
\rho=\sqrt{\frac{n-1}{2n}}.
\]
Choose three points \(z,x,y\in\mathbb R^n\) satisfying
\[
|z-x|=|z-y|=D,\qquad |x-y|=1.
\]
This is possible in a two-dimensional subspace because \(1<2D\).

In the perpendicular bisector hyperplane of \(zx\), place \(n\) points
\[
A=\{a_1,\dots,a_n\}
\]
forming a regular \((n-1)\)-simplex of side one and centered at the midpoint of \(zx\). Its circumradius is \(\rho\). Since
\[
\frac{D^2}{4}+\rho^2
=
\frac{n+1}{2n}+\frac{n-1}{2n}
=1,
\]
each \(a_i\) is at distance one from both \(z\) and \(x\).

Similarly, construct
\[
B=\{b_1,\dots,b_n\}
\]
so that the \(b_i\) form a regular simplex of side one and each is at distance one from \(z\) and \(y\). The second simplex can be oriented so that \(A\) and \(B\) are disjoint. For \(n\ge3\), a generic rotation about the axis \(zy\) avoids the finitely many coincidences. For \(n=2\), one may take
\[
z=(0,0),\quad x=(\sqrt3,0),\quad
y=\left(\frac{5\sqrt3}{6},\frac{\sqrt{33}}6\right),
\]
and the two pairs of circle-intersection points are distinct.

Let \(H_n\) have vertex set
\[
A\cup B\cup\{z,x,y\},
\]
with the following designated unit edges:

- \(A\) is a clique, and every point of \(A\) is adjacent to \(z\) and \(x\);
- \(B\) is a clique, and every point of \(B\) is adjacent to \(z\) and \(y\);
- \(x\) is adjacent to \(y\).

Thus each of \(A\cup\{z,x\}\) and \(B\cup\{z,y\}\) induces a copy of \(K_{n+2}\) with one edge deleted.

To calculate the independence number, let \(I\) be independent.

- If \(I\cap A\ne\varnothing\), then \(I\) contains at most one point from \(A\), cannot contain \(z\) or \(x\), and contains at most one point of the clique \(B\cup\{y\}\). Hence \(|I|\le2\).
- The same holds if \(I\cap B\ne\varnothing\).
- If \(I\) meets neither \(A\) nor \(B\), then \(I\subseteq\{z,x,y\}\), where \(xy\) is an edge, so again \(|I|\le2\).

Also \(\{z,x\}\) is independent because \(|z-x|=D\ne1\). Therefore \(\alpha(H_n)=2\). \(\square\)

### Proof of Theorem A

For the graph \(H_n\),
\[
\frac{|V(H_n)|}{\alpha(H_n)}
=
\frac{2n+3}{2}
=n+\frac32.
\]
If \(|K|=m\le n+1\), then
\[
m<n+\frac32.
\]
Corollary 2.2 therefore gives
\[
\mathbb R^n\to(\ell_2,K).
\]
\(\square\)

The same averaging argument permits vertex weights: if \(w_v\ge0\), with total weight \(W\), and every independent set has weight at most \(A\), then every \(m<W/A\) is forced. Better weighted finite unit-distance graphs would therefore enlarge the universal cardinality range.

## 4. Complete proof for two points in dimension one

Let the two points of \(K\) be at distance \(r\). An avoiding coloring \(c:\mathbb R\to\{R,B\}\) must satisfy

- points at distance \(1\) are not both red;
- points at distance \(r\) are not both blue.

### Necessary periods

Any such coloring has periods \(1+r\) and \(1-r\).

Indeed, if \(c(x)=R\), then \(c(x+1)=B\), hence
\[
c(x+1+r)=c(x+1-r)=R.
\]
If \(c(x)=B\), then \(c(x+r)=c(x-r)=R\), hence
\[
c(x+r+1)=c(x-r+1)=B.
\]
Thus in both cases
\[
c(x+1+r)=c(x),\qquad c(x+1-r)=c(x).
\]

Suppose now that \(r=p/q\) in lowest terms and \(p,q\) have opposite parity. Then \(q+p\) and \(q-p\) are odd, and
\[
\gcd(q+p,q-p)=1.
\]
Since
\[
1+r=\frac{q+p}{q},\qquad 1-r=\frac{q-p}{q}
\]
are periods, Bézout's identity implies that \(1/q\) is a period. Hence both
\[
1=\frac q q,\qquad r=\frac p q
\]
are periods.

If \(c(x)=R\), then \(c(x+1)=R\), a forbidden red unit pair. If \(c(x)=B\), then \(c(x+r)=B\), a forbidden blue \(r\)-pair. Thus no avoiding coloring exists.

### Constructions in the remaining cases

If \(r=p/q\) with \(p,q\) both odd, partition \(\mathbb R\) into cosets of \(q^{-1}\mathbb Z\). On each coset, color
\[
t+\frac{k}{q}
\]
red for even \(k\) and blue for odd \(k\). Both a unit step, which changes \(k\) by \(q\), and an \(r\)-step, which changes \(k\) by \(p\), reverse the color. Thus there is neither a red unit pair nor a blue \(r\)-pair.

If \(r\) is irrational, let
\[
\Gamma=\mathbb Z+\mathbb Zr.
\]
Every element of \(\Gamma\) has a unique representation \(a+br\). On each coset \(t+\Gamma\), color
\[
t+a+br
\]
according to the parity of \(a+b\). Again, adding either \(1\) or \(r\) reverses the color. Choosing representatives of the cosets gives a global avoiding coloring.

This proves Theorem B. For example,
\[
\mathbb R\to(\ell_2,\{0,2\}),
\qquad
\mathbb R\nrightarrow(\ell_2,\{0,1\}),
\qquad
\mathbb R\nrightarrow(\ell_2,\{0,3\}).
\]

## 5. An exact algorithm for rational one-dimensional configurations

Let \(K\subset\mathbb R\) be finite and suppose all pairwise differences in \(K\) are rational. After translating and clearing denominators, write
\[
K=k_0+\frac1q A,
\qquad A\subset\mathbb Z,\quad q\in\mathbb N.
\]

The problem reduces exactly to deciding whether there is a bi-infinite binary sequence \((r_i)_{i\in\mathbb Z}\), where \(1\) denotes red, satisfying for every \(i\),

\[
r_i+r_{i+q}\le1,
\]
\[
\max_{a\in A}r_{i+a}=1,
\qquad
\max_{a\in A}r_{i-a}=1.
\]

The first condition forbids red unit pairs. The latter two say that every translated copy of \(K\), and every translated reflected copy, contains a red point.

These are finite-range shift-of-finite-type constraints. Put
\[
L=\max\left(q,\max_{a\in A}|a|\right).
\]
Construct the finite directed de Bruijn graph whose vertices are binary words of length \(2L\), with an edge when the words overlap in \(2L-1\) positions and the resulting word of length \(2L+1\) satisfies the three constraints at its central coordinate. A legal bi-infinite sequence exists if and only if this finite directed graph contains a directed cycle.

Consequently:

- the arrow relation is decidable for every finite one-dimensional \(K\) with rational pairwise differences;
- whenever it fails, there is an avoiding coloring which is periodic on each \(q^{-1}\mathbb Z\)-coset, and hence may be chosen globally periodic.

This is an exact finite algorithm, though not an efficient one in terms of the bit length of \(q\).

## 6. Somewhere-dense sets admit explicit countercolorings

Suppose
\[
B(c,\rho)\subseteq\overline K
\]
for some \(\rho>0\).

Choose \(a>0\) sufficiently small that
\[
\frac{a\sqrt n}{2}<\rho
\]
and such that \(a^{-2}\notin\mathbb N\). For example, one may take
\[
a=(M+\tfrac12)^{-1/2}
\]
for a sufficiently large integer \(M\).

There is no pair of lattice points in \(a\mathbb Z^n\) at distance one. Moreover,
\[
\Delta=\inf_{z\in\mathbb Z^n}\bigl|a\|z\|-1\bigr|>0.
\]
Indeed, only finitely many lattice vectors have length between \(1/2\) and \(3/2\), and none has length exactly one.

Choose
\[
0<\delta<\frac{\Delta}{3}
\]
and define the open red set
\[
R=\bigcup_{z\in\mathbb Z^n} B(az,\delta).
\]
If \(u,v\in R\), with respective nearby lattice points \(az,aw\), then
\[
\left|\|u-v\|-a\|z-w\|\right|<2\delta<\Delta.
\]
Hence \(\|u-v\|\ne1\). Thus \(R\) has no red unit-distance pair.

Every point of \(\mathbb R^n\) lies within \(a\sqrt n/2<\rho\) of \(a\mathbb Z^n\). Therefore every ball of radius \(\rho\) intersects \(R\) in a nonempty open set.

For any Euclidean isometry \(g\),
\[
B(g(c),\rho)\subseteq\overline{g(K)}.
\]
The open set \(R\cap B(g(c),\rho)\) is nonempty, so it intersects \(g(K)\). Thus every isometric copy of \(K\) contains a red point, and no copy is all blue.

This proves Theorem C.

## 7. Compactness for finite \(K\)

For finite \(K\), the arrow property always has a finite witness.

### Proposition 7.1

For finite \(K\subset\mathbb R^n\),
\[
\mathbb R^n\to(\ell_2,K)
\]
if and only if there is a finite set \(S\subset\mathbb R^n\) such that every red/blue coloring of \(S\) contains either

- a red unit-distance pair in \(S\), or
- a blue isometric copy of \(K\) contained in \(S\).

#### Proof

Introduce one Boolean variable for every point of \(\mathbb R^n\). For every unit pair, impose the finite clause that its two endpoints are not both red. For every isometric copy of finite \(K\), impose the finite clause that at least one of its points is red.

If the full collection is unsatisfiable, propositional compactness gives a finite unsatisfiable subcollection. The union of the points appearing in these finitely many clauses is the required \(S\). The converse is immediate. \(\square\)

This does not itself provide an effective algorithm in arbitrary dimensions, but it shows that every positive result for finite \(K\) is genuinely finitary.

## 8. Remaining gaps

The broad classification remains open in substantial regimes:

1. Theorem A does not decide general finite configurations with \(|K|\ge n+2\). The averaging criterion is only sufficient and ignores the geometry of \(K\).
2. Apart from the two-point case and the rational finite-state reduction, higher-point one-dimensional configurations are not classified here.
3. The countercoloring for \(\operatorname{int}(\overline K)\ne\varnothing\) leaves nowhere-dense infinite sets essentially untouched.
4. For infinite \(K\), the finite compactness argument fails because “this copy of \(K\) contains a red point” is an infinite clause.

I have not independently checked whether the generalized-spindle bound or the one-dimensional parity classification already appears in the Euclidean Ramsey literature; both are elementary enough that prior occurrence is plausible.
