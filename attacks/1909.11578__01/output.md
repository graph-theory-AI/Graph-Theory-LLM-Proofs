# Mathematical writeup

## 1. Conventions and results obtained

I use the standard definition from this area: \(A\subseteq [k]^n\) is **symmetric** if there is a transitive group \(G\leq S_n\) of coordinate permutations with \(gA=A\) for every \(g\in G\). If “symmetric” means full \(S_n\)-invariance, all results below remain valid.

For \(\ell\in[k]\), write
\[
S_\ell(x)=\{i\in[n]:x_i=\ell\}.
\]
A set-intersecting family has the form
\[
A=\{x:S_\ell(x)\in\mathcal U\},
\qquad
\mathcal U=\{S\subseteq[n]:B\subseteq S\text{ for some }B\in\mathcal B\},
\]
where \(\mathcal U\) is an intersecting upset.

The following partial results are proved below.

### Theorem A: exact result for \(n\leq4\)

Let \(k\geq3\) and \(1\leq n\leq4\). Every maximum-cardinality symmetric intersecting family in \([k]^n\) is set-intersecting. More precisely:

- for \(n=1,2\), the maximum is \(1\), attained only by a constant vector;
- for \(n=3,4\), the maximum is
  \[
  1+n(k-1),
  \]
  and every maximum family is, up to the choice of \(\ell\in[k]\),
  \[
  A_\ell=\{x\in[k]^n:|S_\ell(x)|\geq n-1\}.
  \]

For \(n=3,4\), this is generated by
\[
\mathcal B=\binom{[n]}{n-1}.
\]

### Theorem B: a reduction and bound for \(n=5\)

Put
\[
M_{k,5}
 =\sum_{j=3}^{5}\binom5j(k-1)^{5-j}
 =10(k-1)^2+5(k-1)+1.
\]
This is the size of the set-intersecting family
\[
A_\ell=\{x:|S_\ell(x)|\geq3\},
\]
and is also the largest size of a set-intersecting family in \([k]^5\).

If a symmetric intersecting \(A\subseteq[k]^5\) has the property that every \(x\in A\) has some symbol occurring at least three times, then
\[
|A|\leq M_{k,5},
\]
with equality only for one of the families \(A_\ell\).

Thus any counterexample at \(n=5\), including a non-set-intersecting family tied at size \(M_{k,5}\), must contain a cyclic orbit of vectors with multiplicity pattern \((2,2,1)\).

Moreover, for every symmetric intersecting \(A\subseteq[k]^5\),
\[
M_{k,5}\leq \max |A|\leq M_{k,5}+5r_k,
\]
where
\[
r_3=2,\qquad
r_k=\max\{k-2,3\}\quad(k\geq4).
\]

The remaining additive gap comes entirely from the balanced \((2,2,1)\) cyclic necklaces.

## 2. The literal statement is false for \(k=2\)

This is presumably why the intended conjecture should be read with \(k\geq3\).

Identify \([2]\) with \(\{0,1\}\), and let \(n\geq3\) be odd. Define
\[
A_{\mathrm{even}}
 =\left\{x\in\{0,1\}^n:\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\equiv0\pmod2\right\}.
\]

This family is invariant under all coordinate permutations. In the binary cube, two vectors fail to agree in every coordinate precisely when they are complements. Since \(n\) is odd, complementation reverses parity. Hence \(A_{\mathrm{even}}\) contains no complementary pair and is intersecting.

The complementary pairs partition \(\{0,1\}^n\), so every intersecting family has size at most \(2^{n-1}\). Thus \(A_{\mathrm{even}}\), of size \(2^{n-1}\), is maximum even without the symmetry restriction.

It is not set-intersecting. Indeed, in any set-intersecting representation the collection of possible \(\ell\)-supports is an upset. For \(\ell=1\), membership in \(A_{\mathrm{even}}\) means that the support has even cardinality, which is not upward-closed. For \(\ell=0\), the support has odd cardinality, also not upward-closed.

For example, at \(n=3\),
\[
A_{\mathrm{even}}=\{000,011,101,110\}
\]
is an explicit maximum symmetric intersecting family that is not set-intersecting.

## 3. Proof of Theorem A

### 3.1. Small transitive groups contain regular subgroups

Let \(G\leq S_n\) be transitive.

- For \(n=3\), \(3\mid |G|\), so \(G\) contains a \(3\)-cycle. Thus \(G\) contains a regular cyclic subgroup \(C_3\).
- For \(n=4\), every transitive subgroup of \(S_4\) contains a regular subgroup isomorphic to either \(C_4\) or \(V_4\). This follows from the elementary classification
  \[
  C_4,\ V_4,\ D_8,\ A_4,\ S_4
  \]
  of transitive subgroups of degree four: \(D_8\) contains a regular \(C_4\), while \(A_4\) and \(S_4\) contain the regular Klein four group of double transpositions.

Therefore a symmetric family in dimensions three or four is invariant under such a regular subgroup \(H\). For every \(x\in A\), its \(H\)-orbit must itself be intersecting.

### 3.2. Dimension three

Let \(H=C_3\). If \(x\in[k]^3\) has three distinct entries, then \(x\) and either nontrivial cyclic shift disagree in every coordinate. Hence such a vector cannot belong to \(A\).

Thus every member of \(A\) is either constant or has multiplicity pattern \((2,1)\). For \(a\neq b\), let
\[
O_{a,b}=\{x\in[k]^3:x\text{ has two entries }a\text{ and one entry }b\}.
\]
Since the cyclic group is transitive, \(O_{a,b}\) is exactly the cyclic orbit of any such vector.

### 3.3. Dimension four

First suppose \(H=C_4=\langle r\rangle\). For the orbit of \(x\) to be intersecting, \(x\) must agree with both \(rx\) and \(r^2x\).

- Agreement with \(rx\) requires a monochromatic adjacent pair on the coordinate \(4\)-cycle.
- Agreement with \(r^2x\) requires a monochromatic antipodal pair.

If no symbol occurs at least three times, the multiplicity pattern is one of
\[
(2,2),\qquad (2,1,1),\qquad (1,1,1,1).
\]
With multiplicities at most two, an adjacent monochromatic pair and an antipodal monochromatic pair cannot coexist: equal pairs belonging to different symbols are disjoint, while the complement of an adjacent pair is again adjacent. Hence one of \(rx,r^2x\) disagrees with \(x\) everywhere.

Now suppose \(H=V_4\). Its three nonidentity elements correspond to the three perfect matchings of the four coordinates. The vectors \(x\) and \(hx\) agree precisely when the matching corresponding to \(h\) has a monochromatic edge. With multiplicity pattern \((2,2)\), both monochromatic pairs belong to the same perfect matching; with pattern \((2,1,1)\), only one matching has a monochromatic edge; and with all entries distinct, none does. Thus again some translate disagrees with \(x\) everywhere.

Consequently, every \(x\in A\) is constant or has multiplicity pattern \((3,1)\). For \(a\neq b\), write
\[
O_{a,b}=\{x\in[k]^4:x\text{ has three entries }a\text{ and one entry }b\}.
\]
Regularity and transitivity show that this is one \(H\)-orbit.

### 3.4. Compatibility of the possible orbits

The following statements hold for both \(n=3\) and \(n=4\).

1. Two distinct constant vectors are incompatible.

2. The orbits \(O_{a,b}\) and \(O_{c,d}\) are cross-intersecting if and only if \(a=c\).

   If \(a=c\), every vector in either orbit has \(a\) in \(n-1\) positions, and two \((n-1)\)-subsets of \([n]\) intersect.

   Suppose \(a\neq c\). If \(b\neq d\), choose the two exceptional positions to be equal. At that position the values are \(b,d\), and elsewhere they are \(a,c\), so the resulting vectors disagree everywhere.

   If \(b=d=s\), then \(s\notin\{a,c\}\). Choose distinct exceptional positions \(p,q\). At \(p\) the values are \(s,c\), at \(q\) they are \(a,s\), and elsewhere they are \(a,c\). Again there is no agreement.

3. A constant vector \(s^n\) is compatible with \(O_{a,b}\) exactly when \(s\in\{a,b\}\).

It follows that all nonconstant orbits in \(A\) have a common majority symbol \(a\). If \(t\) of the possible orbits \(O_{a,b}\) are included, then \(t\leq k-1\), and at most one constant vector can be included. Hence
\[
|A|\leq nt+1\leq n(k-1)+1.
\]

Equality requires all \(k-1\) orbits \(O_{a,b}\), \(b\neq a\), and one constant. Since \(k\geq3\), compatibility with all these orbits forces the constant to be \(a^n\). Therefore
\[
A=\{a^n\}\cup\bigcup_{b\neq a}O_{a,b}
 =\{x:|S_a(x)|\geq n-1\}.
\]

For \(n=2\), every transitive group contains the coordinate swap. The orbit of a nonconstant vector \((a,b)\) is \(\{(a,b),(b,a)\}\), whose two members disagree in both coordinates. Thus only constant vectors may occur, and at most one of them can be selected. The case \(n=1\) is immediate.

Finally, for \(n=3,4\), taking
\[
\mathcal B=\binom{[n]}{n-1}
\]
gives the asserted set-intersecting representation. This proves Theorem A.

## 4. The exact cyclic reduction in dimension five

Let \(A\subseteq[k]^5\) be symmetric. If \(G\leq S_5\) is a transitive symmetry group, then \(5\mid |G|\), so \(G\) contains a \(5\)-cycle. After relabeling coordinates, \(A\) is invariant under cyclic shifts of \(\mathbb Z_5\).

For \(x\in[k]^{\mathbb Z_5}\), put
\[
S_a(x)=\{i\in\mathbb Z_5:x_i=a\}.
\]
The cyclic orbit of \(x\) is intersecting if and only if
\[
\bigcup_{a\in[k]}\bigl(S_a(x)-S_a(x)\bigr)=\mathbb Z_5. \tag{1}
\]
Indeed, \(x\) and its shift by \(t\) agree exactly when \(t\in S_a(x)-S_a(x)\) for some \(a\).

If some symbol occurs at least three times, then (1) holds because any two \(3\)-subsets of \(\mathbb Z_5\) intersect.

If every multiplicity is at most two, a two-element color class contributes one of the two nonzero difference pairs
\[
\{\pm1\},\qquad \{\pm2\}.
\]
Thus (1) holds precisely for multiplicity pattern \((2,2,1)\), with the two doubleton color classes having different cyclic distances.

Consequently, every admissible nonconstant cyclic orbit has one of the following forms:

- \(4+1\);
- \(3+2\), with two possible cyclic distances for the \(2\)-set;
- \(3+1+1\), with two possible cyclic distances between the singleton positions;
- the balanced \(2+2+1\) pattern just described.

All nonconstant orbits have size five.

## 5. The largest set-intersecting family for \(n=5\)

Let \(\mathcal B\subseteq2^{[5]}\) be symmetric and intersecting. Its transitive symmetry group contains a \(5\)-cycle.

No member of \(\mathcal B\) can have size at most two:

- cyclic translates of a singleton are disjoint;
- if \(|B|=2\), choose \(t\notin B-B\); then \(B\cap(B+t)=\varnothing\).

Thus every witness \(B\in\mathcal B\) has size at least three. Therefore every family generated from \(\mathcal B\) and a symbol \(\ell\) is contained in
\[
\{x:|S_\ell(x)|\geq3\}.
\]
Taking all \(3\)-subsets as witnesses attains this family. Hence the maximum size among set-intersecting families is
\[
M_{k,5}=10(k-1)^2+5(k-1)+1.
\]

## 6. Proof of Theorem B without balanced orbits

Assume every member of \(A\) has a symbol occurring at least three times.

There are five unlabelled nonconstant cyclic equality patterns:

- three patterns with two color classes: one \(4+1\) pattern and two \(3+2\) patterns;
- two patterns with three color classes: the two \(3+1+1\) patterns.

Fix one such equality pattern with \(r\) color classes, and align every cyclic orbit with a fixed canonical representative of the pattern. A labeling of the \(r\) classes is then an injection
\[
f:[r]\hookrightarrow[k].
\]

If two selected cyclic orbits are compatible, their canonical representatives must in particular agree in some coordinate. Since they have the same equality partition, the two injections must agree in at least one class.

Let \(\tau\) be a \(k\)-cycle on the alphabet. The injections
\[
f,\tau f,\ldots,\tau^{k-1}f
\]
form an orbit of size \(k\), and any two distinct members disagree at every class because every nonidentity power of a \(k\)-cycle is fixed-point-free. Thus at most one can be selected. There are therefore at most
\[
\frac{(k)_r}{k}
\]
selected cyclic orbits of a fixed equality pattern.

For \(r=2\), this is \(k-1\); for \(r=3\), it is \((k-1)(k-2)\). Hence the number of nonconstant cyclic orbits is at most
\[
3(k-1)+2(k-1)(k-2)
 =(k-1)(2k-1).
\]
At most one constant vector may be present. Therefore
\[
|A|
 \leq 1+5(k-1)(2k-1)
 =10(k-1)^2+5(k-1)+1
 =M_{k,5}.
\]

### Equality

For either a \(4+1\) or fixed \(3+2\) pattern, two labeled orbits are compatible if and only if their majority labels coincide.

Indeed, if their majority labels differ and their minority labels differ, their canonical representatives disagree everywhere. If the minority label is common, cyclically shift one minority block to be disjoint from the other; all positions then have different symbols.

Suppose equality holds in the preceding bound. Then:

- one constant vector \(s^5\) is selected;
- all three two-class pattern bounds are attained;
- both three-class pattern bounds are attained.

For each two-class pattern, attainment means all \(k-1\) choices of the minority label occur with one common majority label. Compatibility with \(s^5\) forces that majority label to be \(s\): if it were \(a\neq s\), then because \(k\geq3\) one of the minority labels could be chosen outside \(\{a,s\}\), giving a word with no occurrence of \(s\).

Now consider a selected \(3+1+1\) orbit. Compatibility with \(s^5\) requires that \(s\) occur in it. If \(s\) were a singleton symbol, align this singleton with the exceptional position of a \(4+1\) word having majority \(s\). At that position the two symbols differ, while everywhere else the \(4+1\) word has symbol \(s\) and the \(3+1+1\) word does not. This gives a pair disagreeing everywhere.

Thus \(s\) must be the threefold symbol in every selected \(3+1+1\) orbit. Attainment of the bound then forces all choices of the other two distinct labels. It follows that
\[
A=\{x:|S_s(x)|\geq3\}.
\]

This proves Theorem B under the exclusion of balanced \((2,2,1)\) orbits.

## 7. Bounding the remaining balanced orbits

There is one unlabelled admissible balanced pattern. Use the canonical partition
\[
P_A=\{1,4\},\qquad
P_B=\{2,3\},\qquad
P_C=\{0\}.
\]
Write \(R(a,b,c)\) for the cyclic orbit assigning distinct labels \(a,b,c\) to \(P_A,P_B,P_C\), respectively.

The relevant difference sets are
\[
\begin{aligned}
P_A-P_A&=\{0,\pm2\},&
P_B-P_B&=\{0,\pm1\},&
P_C-P_C&=\{0\},\\
P_A-P_B&=P_B-P_A=\mathbb Z_5\setminus\{0\},\\
P_A-P_C&=P_C-P_A=\{\pm1\},&
P_B-P_C&=P_C-P_B=\{\pm2\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
It follows directly that \(R(a,b,c)\) and \(R(a',b',c')\) are compatible exactly when
\[
\bigl(a=a'\ \text{and}\ b=b'\bigr)
\quad\text{or}\quad
\bigl(c=c'\ \text{and}\ (a=b'\ \text{or}\ b=a')\bigr). \tag{2}
\]

From (2), the maximum number of pairwise compatible balanced orbits is
\[
r_3=2,\qquad r_k=\max\{k-2,3\}\quad(k\geq4).
\]

To see this, if two selected triples have the same ordered pair \((a,b)\) but different \(c\)'s, no triple with another ordered pair can be compatible with both; this gives at most \(k-2\) triples.

Otherwise all ordered pairs \((a,b)\) are distinct. Any two selected triples then have a common \(c\), and the ordered pairs form directed edges satisfying
\[
(a,b)\sim(a',b')
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
a=b'\text{ or }b=a'.
\]
Fixing one directed edge shows that there can be at most one further edge starting at its head and at most one further edge ending at its tail, so there are at most three edges. A directed triangle attains three when \(k\geq4\); for \(k=3\), a reversed pair attains two.

Combining this with the previous five-pattern bounds gives
\[
|A|\leq M_{k,5}+5r_k.
\]

## 8. Remaining gap

The intended conjecture for \(k\geq3\) remains unresolved.

The first unresolved dimension is \(n=5\). There the issue is now isolated:

- every set-intersecting family has size at most \(M_{k,5}\);
- every symmetric intersecting family avoiding balanced \((2,2,1)\) necklaces has size at most \(M_{k,5}\), with equality only for the strict-majority construction;
- a counterexample at \(n=5\) must contain one of the balanced cyclic orbits \(R(a,b,c)\);
- at most \(r_k=O(k)\) such balanced orbits can occur, leaving an additive gap of at most \(5r_k\).

For example, the cyclic orbit of
\[
(3,1,2,2,1)
\]
is internally intersecting: the two positions carrying \(1\) contribute differences \(\{0,\pm2\}\), while the two positions carrying \(2\) contribute \(\{0,\pm1\}\). This shows exactly why the \(n\leq4\) majority-orbit argument cannot simply be extended. Its orbit has only five members, so it is not itself extremal.
