# Partial result

Let
\[
\Phi(t):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
 \frac{(-t)^k}{k!\,2^{\binom{k}{2}}},
\]
and let \(\rho\) be its first positive zero. Then
\[
1.488<\rho<1.489,\qquad \rho^{-1}\approx 0.67201.
\]

I prove:

**Theorem.** For every \(\varepsilon>0\),
\[
\Pr\!\left(
 \operatorname{HG}(G(n,1/2))
 \le (\rho^{-1}+\varepsilon)n
\right)\longrightarrow 1.
\]
In particular,
\[
\operatorname{HG}(G(n,1/2))
 \le (0.673+o(1))n
\qquad\text{with high probability}.
\]

An entirely explicit, slightly weaker consequence is
\[
\operatorname{HG}(G(n,1/2))
 \le \left(\frac{25}{37}+o(1)\right)n
 \approx 0.675676\,n
\]
with high probability.

This rules out a hat guessing number asymptotic to \(n\), but it does not settle whether the answer is \(o(n)\).

---

## 1. Correct-guess events have dependency graph \(G\)

Fix a graph \(G=(V,E)\), a palette of \(q\) colors, and an arbitrary deterministic guessing strategy. Give every vertex an independent uniformly random color \(X_v\in[q]\). For \(v\in V\), let
\[
A_v=\{X_v=f_v(X_{N(v)})\}
\]
be the event that \(v\) guesses correctly. Then
\[
\Pr(A_v)=\frac1q.
\]

More importantly, \(G\) is a dependency graph for the family \((A_v)_{v\in V}\). Indeed, let
\[
U\subseteq V\setminus N[v].
\]
Every event \(A_u\), \(u\in U\), is independent of the coordinate \(X_v\), because \(uv\notin E\). Thus any event \(B\) in the sigma-algebra generated by \((A_u)_{u\in U}\) does not involve \(X_v\). Conditioning on all coordinates except \(X_v\), exactly one of the \(q\) choices of \(X_v\) makes \(A_v\) occur. Consequently,
\[
\Pr(A_v\cap B)=\frac1q\Pr(B)=\Pr(A_v)\Pr(B).
\]

This observation is uniform over all guessing strategies.

## 2. A deterministic independence-polynomial obstruction

For a graph \(H\), let \(i_k(H)\) denote its number of independent \(k\)-vertex sets, and let
\[
I(H,x)=\sum_{k\ge0}i_k(H)x^k
\]
be its independence polynomial.

We use the following self-contained form of Shearer's lemma.

### Lemma 1

Let \((A_v)_{v\in V(H)}\) be events having dependency graph \(H\), with
\[
\Pr(A_v)\le z.
\]
If
\[
I(H[S],-z)>0
\qquad\text{for every }S\subseteq V(H),
\]
then
\[
\Pr\left(\bigcap_{v\in V(H)}\overline{A_v}\right)>0.
\]

### Proof

More generally, allow probabilities \(p_v\), and define
\[
Q(S):=
\sum_{\substack{J\subseteq S\\J\text{ independent}}}
 (-1)^{|J|}\prod_{j\in J}p_j.
\]
For \(i\in S\),
\[
Q(S)=Q(S\setminus\{i\})-p_iQ(S\setminus N[i]).
\tag{1}
\]

Write
\[
E_S=\bigcap_{j\in S}\overline{A_j},
\qquad P(S)=\Pr(E_S).
\]
We prove inductively that for \(T\subseteq S\),
\[
\frac{P(S)}{P(T)}\ge \frac{Q(S)}{Q(T)}.
\tag{2}
\]
Suppose first that \(T=S\setminus\{i\}\). Since \(A_i\) is independent of all events indexed by \(S\setminus N[i]\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(A_i\mid E_{S\setminus\{i\}})
&\le
\frac{\Pr(A_i\cap E_{S\setminus N[i]})}
     {P(S\setminus\{i\})}\\
&\le
p_i\frac{P(S\setminus N[i])}
          {P(S\setminus\{i\})}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The induction hypothesis, applied inside \(S\setminus\{i\}\), gives
\[
\frac{P(S\setminus N[i])}{P(S\setminus\{i\})}
\le
\frac{Q(S\setminus N[i])}{Q(S\setminus\{i\})}.
\]
Therefore, using (1),
\[
\frac{P(S)}{P(S\setminus\{i\})}
\ge
1-p_i\frac{Q(S\setminus N[i])}{Q(S\setminus\{i\})}
=
\frac{Q(S)}{Q(S\setminus\{i\})}.
\]
Multiplication along a chain from \(T\) to \(S\) proves (2). Taking \(T=\varnothing\) yields
\[
P(S)\ge Q(S)>0.
\]
For equal probabilities \(p_v=z\), one has \(Q(S)=I(H[S],-z)\). ∎

It follows immediately that:

### Corollary 2

If
\[
I(G[S],-1/q)>0
\qquad\text{for every }S\subseteq V(G),
\tag{3}
\]
then no strategy on \(G\) can guarantee a correct guess with \(q\) colors.

Indeed, Lemma 1 gives positive probability that none of the vertices guesses correctly, and hence at least one losing coloring exists.

## 3. Uniform independence-polynomial asymptotics for \(G(n,1/2)\)

The key random-graph estimate is the following.

### Lemma 3

Fix \(c>0\). For \(G\sim G(n,1/2)\), with high probability,
\[
\sup_{\substack{S\subseteq V(G)\\0\le z\le c/n}}
\left|
 I(G[S],-z)-\Phi(z|S|)
\right|=o(1).
\tag{4}
\]

### Proof

Fix an integer \(k\ge2\). For a fixed \(S\), with \(s=|S|\),
\[
\mathbb E\,i_k(G[S])
=
2^{-\binom{k}{2}}\binom{s}{k}.
\tag{5}
\]

Changing a single edge of \(G\) changes \(i_k(G[S])\) by at most
\[
\binom{n-2}{k-2}\le n^{k-2}.
\]
Hence the bounded-differences inequality gives
\[
\Pr\left(
 \left|i_k(G[S])-\mathbb E i_k(G[S])\right|
 > n^{k-1/4}
\right)
\le 2\exp\bigl(-\Omega_k(n^{3/2})\bigr).
\]
A union bound over all \(2^n\) choices of \(S\), and then over any fixed finite collection of \(k\)'s, shows that with high probability,
\[
\max_{S\subseteq V(G)}
\left|
i_k(G[S])
-
2^{-\binom{k}{2}}\binom{|S|}{k}
\right|
=O(n^{k-1/4})
\tag{6}
\]
simultaneously for every fixed \(k\).

Let \(z\le c/n\), \(s=|S|\), and \(t=zs\le c\). From (6), for every fixed truncation level \(L\),
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{L}(-1)^ki_k(G[S])z^k
=
\sum_{k=0}^{L}
 \frac{(-t)^k}{k!\,2^{\binom{k}{2}}}
+o(1)
\tag{7}
\]
uniformly over \(S\) and \(z\). Here we also used
\[
\binom{s}{k}z^k=\frac{(sz)^k}{k!}+O_k(n^{-1}).
\]

The tails are uniformly bounded by
\[
\sum_{k>L}i_k(G[S])z^k
\le
\sum_{k>L}\binom{s}{k}z^k
\le
\sum_{k>L}\frac{c^k}{k!}.
\]
The same bound applies to the tail of \(\Phi(t)\). First choose \(L\) so that this tail is arbitrarily small, and then let \(n\to\infty\) in (7). This proves (4). ∎

## 4. Evaluation of the constant

Write
\[
a_k(t)=\frac{t^k}{k!\,2^{\binom{k}{2}}}.
\]
For \(t\le1.489\), the terms \(a_k(t)\) decrease from \(k=1\) onward. Therefore the alternating-series estimates give
\[
\Phi(t)\ge
P_3(t):=1-t+\frac{t^2}{4}-\frac{t^3}{48}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
P_3'(t)=-1+\frac t2-\frac{t^2}{16}
=-\frac{(t-4)^2}{16}\le0.
\]
At \(t=37/25=1.48\),
\[
P_3(37/25)=\frac{47}{750000}>0.
\]
Thus
\[
\Phi(t)>0\qquad(0\le t\le1.48).
\tag{8}
\]

On the other hand, the alternating-series upper bound gives
\[
\Phi(t)\le
P_4(t):=
1-t+\frac{t^2}{4}-\frac{t^3}{48}+\frac{t^4}{1536}.
\]
At \(t=1489/1000\),
\[
P_4(t)
=
-\frac{455291879359}{1536\cdot10^{12}}<0.
\tag{9}
\]
Therefore the first positive zero satisfies
\[
1.488<\rho<1.489.
\]

The rapidly convergent series gives numerically
\[
\rho\approx1.48807855,
\qquad
\rho^{-1}\approx0.67200754.
\]

It is also useful to note the identity
\[
\Phi'(t)=-\Phi(t/2),
\]
so the first zero is crossed with negative derivative.

## 5. Conclusion for the hat guessing number

Fix any \(c<\rho\). Since \(\Phi\) is positive on the compact interval \([0,c]\), there is a \(\delta_c>0\) such that
\[
\Phi(t)\ge\delta_c\qquad(0\le t\le c).
\]
Lemma 3 therefore implies that, with high probability,
\[
I(G[S],-z)>0
\]
simultaneously for every \(S\subseteq V(G)\) and every \(0\le z\le c/n\).

Now suppose a strategy uses \(q\ge n/c\) colors. Then \(z=1/q\le c/n\), so Corollary 2 supplies a coloring on which every player guesses incorrectly. Thus, with high probability,
\[
\operatorname{HG}(G)<\frac nc.
\]
Letting \(c\uparrow\rho\) proves
\[
\operatorname{HG}(G(n,1/2))
\le(\rho^{-1}+o(1))n.
\]

Taking \(c=37/25\) directly gives the explicit bound
\[
\operatorname{HG}(G(n,1/2))
\le\left(\frac{25}{37}+o(1)\right)n.
\]

---

## 6. Remaining gap

The argument gives only a fixed constant improvement over the trivial upper bound. It does **not** imply
\[
\operatorname{HG}(G(n,1/2))=o(n).
\]

There is also a genuine limitation to this particular route: for \(c>\rho\) sufficiently close to \(\rho\),
\[
I(G,-c/n)\longrightarrow \Phi(c)<0
\]
in probability, so the all-induced-subgraphs positivity condition required by the Shearer argument already fails for \(S=V(G)\). Any proof of sublinearity must therefore use additional structure of hat-guessing events beyond their dependency graph and their marginal probability \(1/q\).
