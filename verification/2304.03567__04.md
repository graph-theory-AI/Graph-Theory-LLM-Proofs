## Interpretation

I began from the default assumption that the writeup games the interpretation, since the
writeup itself flags this risk ("the source definition should be checked for an omitted
qualification"). I downloaded the full TeX source of arXiv:2304.03567v2 from arXiv
(e-print tarball) and read the definitions verbatim:

- `body/definitions.tex`, line 8: "An arc $xy$ of $D$ is *forward* if $x<y$. We say
  that a couple $(x,y)$ is *connected* in $(D,<)$ if there is a directed path from $x$
  to $y$ consisting of forward arcs, and we then call $(x,y)$ a *forward couple*."
- `body/request.tex`, line 47: "Let us call a *forward cover* of $D$ a set of vertex
  orderings $\leq_1,\dots,\leq_k$ such that for every pair $\{x,y\}$, there is some $i$
  such that $x,y$ or $y,x$ is a forward pair in $\leq_i$."
- `body/request.tex`, lines 49-51 (Conjecture 1, verbatim): "Every strong digraph $D$
  on $n$ vertices has a $O(\log n)$ size forward cover."

The writeup's formalization (a pair {x,y} is covered by an ordering iff there is a
directed path between them, in either direction, whose arcs all go forward in the
ordering; a forward cover must cover all unordered pairs) is **exactly** the paper's
definition — no hypothesis is strengthened, weakened, or reinterpreted. A path of
forward arcs is automatically increasing along the ordering, so the writeup's
"$v_0 \prec v_1 \prec \cdots \prec v_\ell$" phrasing is equivalent. The catalog
statement also matches the paper verbatim. This is not a strawman reading: a reasonable
author of the source paper would consider Conjecture 1 resolved (negatively) by this
construction. Interpretation: **faithful**.

Notably, the paper itself observes (`request.tex` line 11) that for *ordered* couples
the circuit with reversed requests kills any constant ratio, and adds (line 13):
"Surprisingly, when requests are pairs, we could not find any set of request $R$ which
is not satisfied within a ratio of $1/\log n$." The writeup's construction is precisely
the gadget the authors were missing: it splits each hard request into twin vertices
$a_i, b_i$ with identical in/out-neighborhoods, so that *both* orientations of the pair
force the same wrap-around of the cycle. That sentence is an informal remark, not a
theorem, so nothing proven in the paper is contradicted (see Caveats).

## Computational check

Script: `verification/scripts/2304.03567__04/check_fast.py`
(pure Python 3 standard library, no dependencies; runs in ~14 s). Encoding: vertices
$c_i,a_i,b_i \mapsto 3i, 3i{+}1, 3i{+}2$; forward reachability by DFS restricted to
arcs $u\to v$ with $\mathrm{pos}(u)<\mathrm{pos}(v)$.

Exact numbers obtained:

1. **Basic properties** ($m=3,4,5,6,10,20$): $|V|=3m$; strongly connected; no digons,
   no loops, no parallel arcs. All pass.
2. **Exhaustive brute force, $m=3$ ($n=9$)**: all $9! = 362{,}880$ orderings tested.
   Maximum number of hard pairs $\{a_i,b_i\}$ forward-covered by a single ordering:
   **1**. Orderings covering $\ge 2$ hard pairs: **0**. Each of $R_0,R_1,R_2$ is
   individually coverable (explicit witness orderings printed, e.g.
   $a_0,c_1,a_1,b_1,c_2,c_0,b_0,a_2,b_2$ covers $R_0$). Hence the minimum number of
   orderings covering $\{R_0,R_1,R_2\}$ is exactly $3 = n/3$.
3. **Exhaustive Lemma check, $m=3$**: over all $9!$ orderings, every ordering covering
   $R_i$ satisfies $c_j \prec c_{j+1}$ for all $j\ne i$ — **0 violations**. Each
   $R_i$ is covered by exactly **6048** orderings $= 2\cdot 9!/5!$, the exact count
   predicted by the forced chain $a_i\prec c_{i+1}\prec c_{i+2}\prec c_i\prec b_i$
   (both directions), a strong consistency check on the encoding.
4. **Symbolic Corollary check, $m=3..6$**: enumerated *all* simple directed paths
   between $a_i$ and $b_i$ in both directions (18 / 54 / 162 / 486 paths per pair for
   $m=3/4/5/6$; in general $2\cdot 3^{m-1}$). For every unordered pair $i\ne k$ and
   every combination of one realizing path for $R_i$ and one for $R_k$, the union of
   their chain constraints was tested for acyclicity: **every combination contains a
   directed cycle**, i.e. no linear order can make both paths forward. This verifies
   the Corollary exhaustively (independently of the Lemma's reasoning) for $m\le 6$.
5. **Randomized + targeted check, $m=10$ and $m=20$** ($n=30, 60$): 20,000 uniform
   random orderings each, plus the $m$ targeted witness orderings: max hard pairs
   covered by any ordering = **1**; each targeted ordering covers exactly its own
   $R_i$.

No claimed property failed. The finite construction behaves exactly as the writeup
asserts.

## Caveats

1. **Parity/divisibility**: the construction gives $n=3m$; for $n$ not divisible by 3
   the bound extends trivially (subdivide one arc or add pendant structure), and the
   asymptotic refutation of $O(\log n)$ needs only infinitely many $n$. Not a gap.
2. **$m\ge 3$ is needed** for the "simple oriented graph" side-claim ($m=2$ creates the
   digon $c_0\leftrightarrow c_1$); the writeup imposes $m\ge 3$ explicitly.
3. **General $m$**: my exhaustive checks cover $m\le 6$ (plus randomized $m=10,20$).
   For general $m$ the Lemma rests on the induction "from $c_j$ every path to $c_i$
   passes next through $c_{j+1}$", which I re-derived and which is transparently
   correct; the path enumeration confirms it structurally for every checked $m$.
4. **Tension with the source paper's informal remark**: `request.tex` line 13 states
   the authors "could not find any set of request $R$ which is not satisfied within a
   ratio of $1/\log n$". The verified construction supplies exactly such an $R$ (ratio
   $1/m$ on $3m$ vertices). Since that sentence reports a failed search rather than a
   theorem, the counterexample contradicts no proved result in the paper — but this
   tension is precisely why I verified the construction three independent ways before
   accepting it. It also means the paper's Proposition 4 is superseded in strength
   (ratio $\Theta(1/n)$ vs $\Theta(1/\log n)$, on linearly many vertices vs
   exponentially many).
5. **What survives of Conjecture 1**: the bi-oriented case proved in the paper is
   untouched ($D_m$ is an oriented graph). The counterexample shows the conjecture
   fails already for simple oriented strong digraphs; any repaired conjecture would
   need to exclude structures like twin "diamond" detours (e.g., restrict to
   bi-oriented or high-connectivity digraphs).
6. **Novelty is not certified**: no citing paper and no published refutation was found
   (searches of September 2026), so I mark this CONFIRMED rather than ALREADY_KNOWN;
   however, the gadget is simple enough that it could exist unpublished or in a
   different guise. Correctness, not novelty, is what this review certifies.
7. The writeup's own front-matter caveat ("the source definition should be checked for
   an omitted qualification") has now been discharged: the source TeX contains no
   additional qualification; the extracted statement is the paper's statement.

## Referee summary

I set out to find the error and could not: the interpretation is verbatim-faithful to
the source paper's definitions (checked against the arXiv TeX source, not the catalog),
every step of the two-page argument is valid, no external results are invoked, and the
finite construction was verified computationally three independent ways, including an
exhaustive check of all 362,880 orderings for $m=3$ and an exhaustive
all-realizing-paths consistency check for $m\le 6$, with exact counts (6048 covering
orderings per pair) matching theory. The chain-of-diamonds digraph $D_m$ ($n=3m$
vertices, strongly connected, oriented, no digons) contains $m$ disjoint "twin" pairs
$\{a_i,b_i\}$ of which any single vertex ordering forward-connects at most one, so any
forward cover needs at least $n/3$ orderings — refuting the $O(\log n)$ bound of
Conjecture 1 in arXiv:2304.03567 and simultaneously giving an RFCPP instance with
realizable ratio $\Theta(1/n)$, far below the paper's own $\Theta(1/\log n)$ lower
bound and contrary to the authors' remark that no sub-$1/\log n$ pair-request family
was known. No published refutation of the conjecture was found, so this appears to be a
correct and possibly new disproof; the authors should be consulted regarding novelty.
