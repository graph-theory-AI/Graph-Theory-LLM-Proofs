## Interpretation

Mohar's exact text (arXiv:1710.11281, Section 3, verified from the TeX source):

> "Let $S$ be a Riemannian surface. Again, we can place $k$ cops and a robber on $S$ and ask whether the cops can capture the robber if they all move with velocity at most 1 [...] and the goal is to get a cop at distance less than $\tfrac12$ from the robber (when we declare that the robber is caught). The cops and the robber know positions of each other at any given time."
>
> Question (bullet 2): "If the cops have a strategy to catch the robber, could it in fact be achieved that one of the cops comes arbitrarily close to the robber (or even onto the same point) in the continuous version of the game?"

The writeup formalizes this as: for the same number $k$ of cops, does winning the radius-$\frac12$ capture game imply a strategy forcing $\inf_t d(C(t),R(t)) = 0$? It then takes $k=1$ on a round sphere of radius $\rho = \frac{1}{4\pi}$ (intrinsic diameter $\frac14 < \frac12$): capture is achieved **at time 0 with no pursuit whatsoever**, while an antipodal delayed-shadowing robber keeps distance $\ge \frac18$ from the single cop forever.

This is a defensible parsing of the sentence, and the writeup is unusually candid about what it is doing ("literal unnormalized formulation", `would_publish: false`, explicit caveats). But it is not the intended question, for four concrete reasons:

1. **The hypothesis is satisfied only vacuously.** On the counterexample, "the cops have a strategy to catch the robber" means the robber is born caught ($d \le \frac14 < \frac12$ at $t=0$, whatever anyone does). The question asks whether *winning pursuit strategies* can be strengthened; the counterexample never engages a pursuit. The family of surfaces on which this trick works is exactly those of diameter $< \frac12$ — precisely the surfaces where the hypothesis is degenerate. (Indeed, on any round sphere of diameter $\ge \frac12$, one cop cannot catch at all — the robber antipodally shadows at distance $=$ diameter — so the $k=1$ premise never holds non-degenerately on a sphere.)
2. **Mohar normalizes scale when scale matters.** Bullet 4 of the *same list* reads: "What is the supremum of the cop numbers taken over all surfaces whose cut locus is at least 1 and whose area is at most $\alpha$?" The constant $\frac12$ is an arbitrary capture convention; Mohar demonstrably knew that unnormalized metric scale trivializes such questions and restricted accordingly where he cared. Under any scale-aware reformulation (injectivity radius $\ge 1$, or "wins the radius-$\varepsilon$ game for every $\varepsilon > 0$"), the counterexample evaporates.
3. **The author's own later work treats the question as live and hard.** Mohar's formal follow-up (arXiv:2205.11633, Canad. J. Math.) redefines the game intrinsically, and Iršič–Mohar–Wesolek (arXiv:2402.05753, 2024) publish, as a main theorem, that *two* cops come arbitrarily close on hyperbolic surfaces of any genus — advertised as a partial answer to this convergence question. That would be pointless if the question admitted a one-line negative answer by shrinking the surface below the capture radius. A reasonable author — here we know the author's subsequent behavior — would not consider the question resolved by this writeup.
4. **Even the literal reading needs the $k=1$ instantiation.** For $k \ge 2$ cops on the same tiny sphere the writeup proves nothing about convergence (it only shows $c_{\mathrm{prox}} \ge 2$); the refutation of "the cops ... one of the cops" rests on reading "the cops" as "exactly the witnessing cop set, of size 1".

So: the formalization is a literal reading, the refutation is real for that reading, but the verdict "disproved" for catalog item 1710.11281__02 constitutes interpretation gaming. The intended question is untouched, as the writeup's own scope section half-concedes.

## Caveats

1. **Degenerate hypothesis (the central caveat).** The counterexample's "winning capture strategy" is the empty strategy: $d \le \frac14 < \frac12$ already at $t = 0$. The disproof works only on surfaces of diameter $< \frac12$, i.e. exactly where "catching" is vacuous; it never refutes the strengthening of any *actual* pursuit strategy.
2. **Scale-invariance mismatch, conceded by the writeup itself.** Capture-radius $\frac12$ is not scale-invariant; "equal-speed antipodal shadowing defeats one cop" is. Any normalization (injectivity radius $\ge 1$ as in Mohar's own bullet 4, curvature/diameter bounds, or "wins radius-$\varepsilon$ capture for every $\varepsilon$") kills the counterexample.
3. **"The cops" read as "exactly one cop".** The refutation needs the witnessing cop set to have size 1. For $k \ge 2$ on the same sphere nothing is shown about convergence; if the question is read as "some bounded number of cops converge" (the reading under which Iršič–Mohar–Wesolek call their two-cop theorem a partial answer), the counterexample says nothing.
4. **Placement convention is load-bearing.** The argument needs cops to place first (standard, and assumed by the writeup). Under a robber-places-first convention the cop could start on the robber's point and $\inf_t d = 0$ trivially. Not a flaw, but unstated as a dependency.
5. **Continuous-strategy formalization chosen silently.** Mohar explicitly writes that the continuous version "needs some justification what a strategy means. Is it just the limit when the time units of discrete game tend to 0?" The writeup picks one formalization (causal maps with the robber's strict delay). This particular choice is robust — the delayed strategy also works in Mohar's discrete day/night version — but the writeup does not discuss the discrete variant at all.
6. **What is actually proven vs. the verdict line.** Proven: $c_{1/2} = 1 < 2 \le c_{\mathrm{prox}}$ for one specific sphere of diameter $\frac14$, under the same-$k$, unnormalized, cops-first, continuous causal formalization. Announced (JSON): "disproved" for the catalog question. The gap between these is the whole finding.
7. The delayed-shadowing-by-a-fixed-point-free-isometry technique is standard/folklore in pursuit games (isometric shadowing on flat tori appears in the geodesic-space cops-and-robber literature); the writeup does not claim novelty for it, and none should be attributed.
