## Interpretation

Problem 5.40 of arXiv:1812.02420 (Hochstättler, Schröder, Steiner) asks, for $k \ge b$,
whether there is a digraph $\vec K(k,b)$ on vertex set $\binom{[k]}{b}$ such that the
induced subdigraph on any subfamily is acyclic iff the subfamily has nonempty
intersection. The writeup answers with a complete classification: such a digraph exists
iff $b \le 2$ or $k \le b+1$; in particular $(k,b)=(5,3)$ is impossible, so the answer
to the open problem is "no" in general.

The only convention on which the verdict could hinge is whether digons (antiparallel
arc pairs) are allowed and count as directed cycles. I fetched the source paper
(https://arxiv.org/html/1812.02420): it states explicitly that digraphs are
"considered loopless, but are allowed to have multiple parallel and anti-parallel
edges/arcs between vertices" and that a "directed cycle of length two (a digon) is a
pair of anti-parallel arcs". The writeup's stated convention ("loopless, antiparallel
arcs allowed, a directed 2-cycle counts as a directed cycle") is therefore *exactly*
the source paper's convention, not a strawman. Indeed the problem is only nontrivially
open under this convention: any two disjoint $b$-sets must induce a non-acyclic
2-vertex digraph, which forces digons whenever $k \ge 2b$ — so the authors plainly
intended digons. Note also that the paper's own confirmed case for $b=1,k=2$ would be
the digon $C_2$.

The problem asks a yes/no question for given $(k,b)$; the writeup resolves it for
every pair of positive integers, negatively in general. A reasonable author of the
source paper would consider Problem 5.40 resolved (in the negative — the hoped-for
tie between fractional dichromatic number and circular homomorphisms via these
digraphs cannot be set up this way for $b \ge 3$, $k \ge b+2$). Interpretation is
fair. The writeup restricts to positive integers, which matches the intent
(see Caveats for the degenerate $b=0$ reading).

## Reference check

The writeup invokes no external theorems. The only reference is the source paper
itself, which I fetched (https://arxiv.org/html/1812.02420, journal version DMTCS
vol. 22 no. 1, 2020):

- **Problem statement**: the paper's concluding open problem asks exactly the
  quoted question (digraph on $\binom{[k]}{b}$, induced subfamily acyclic iff
  nonempty intersection). Confirmed verbatim.
- **$k=b+1$ case**: the paper states "whenever $k=b+1$, such a digraph is given by
  the directed cycle of length $b+1$ whose vertices are associated with the sets
  $\{1,\dots,b+1\}\setminus\{i\}$" — matching the writeup's §3.1. Confirmed.
- **Digraph conventions**: the paper explicitly allows anti-parallel arcs and defines
  a digon as a directed cycle of length two. The writeup's load-bearing convention is
  the paper's own. Confirmed.

Prior-work search (WebSearch, plus the catalog's own auto-review of 2026-05-15):
no paper constructing or refuting these directed Kneser graphs was found; the related
papers arXiv:2309.16565 and arXiv:2511.12553 study dichromatic numbers of Kneser-type
digraphs but not the intersection-acyclicity property. I could not find this
classification in the literature, so ALREADY_KNOWN does not apply on present evidence.

## Computational check

Scripts in `verification/scripts/1812.02420__03/`
(pure Python 3, no external packages; total runtime under 10 seconds):

1. **`check_53_impossible.py`** — an *independent* finite proof of the key negative
   case $(5,3)$, not relying on the writeup's lemma. It verifies the set-level facts
   (every two 3-subsets of $[5]$ intersect, so no digon may appear anywhere; the
   family $\mathcal S_1=\{[5]\setminus\{1,x\}\}$ has empty intersection but all its
   triples intersect; each $\{V_x,V_y,U_{xy}\}$ with $U_{xy}=\{1,x,y\}$ has empty
   intersection with nonempty pairwise intersections). It then enumerates **all
   $2^{12}=4096$ digraphs** on the 4 vertices of $\mathcal S_1$ subject to the
   subfamily constraints: exactly **6 survive, all directed Hamilton 4-cycles**
   (independently confirming the lemma's prediction), each with an arcless pair; and
   enumerates all $2^4$ arc-configurations on $\{V_x,V_y,U_{xy}\}$ given no
   $V_x$–$V_y$ arcs and no digons: **0 of them contain a directed cycle**, so the
   empty-intersection family $\{V_x,V_y,U_{xy}\}$ would be acyclic — contradiction.
   Hence no $\vec K(5,3)$ exists. It also enumerates all inclusion-minimal
   empty-intersection families of $\binom{[5]}{3}$: **30 of size 3 and 5 of size 4**,
   exactly the minimal edge covers of $K_5$, as the writeup's §2 correspondence implies.
2. **`check_constructions.py`** — exhaustive verification of the iff-property over
   *every* nonempty subfamily for: the directed cycle for $k=b+1$, $b=1,\dots,5$;
   the complete bidirected digraph for $b=1$, $k\le5$; and the writeup's $b=2$
   cyclic-order construction for $k=2,\dots,6$ (up to $2^{15}-1=32767$ families).
   **Zero violations.**
3. **`check_b2_k7.py`** — bitmask-based exhaustive check of the $b=2$ construction
   for $k=7$: all $2^{21}-1 = 2\,097\,151$ nonempty families. **Zero violations.**
4. **`check_section2_covers.py`** — verifies for $n=5,6,7$ the §2 correspondence
   (complement bijection; empty intersection $\Leftrightarrow$ edge cover — exhaustive
   for $n=5$, 20000 random families for $n=6,7$), that $\mathcal S_v$ and $\mathcal T$
   are inclusion-minimal edge covers of sizes $n-1$ and $n-2$, and the edge-count
   clash $n-5 \ne n-4$ (0 vs 1, 1 vs 2, 2 vs 3).

All claimed properties passed; the crucial finite counterexample case $(5,3)$ is
confirmed impossible by a computation independent of the writeup's argument.

## Caveats

- **Digon convention (declared by the writeup).** The whole classification lives in
  the source paper's convention (digons allowed and cyclic). Under a digon-free
  convention the problem is trivially false for all $k\ge2b$ (and for $b=1,k\ge2$),
  so no reasonable reading changes the negative answer; the writeup flags this
  correctly in its caveats field.
- **$b=0$.** If $\mathbb N$ were read as containing $0$, the pair $(k,0)$ fails
  trivially (the single vertex $\varnothing$ gives an empty intersection but a
  1-vertex loopless digraph is acyclic), and the writeup's "$k=b$ works" would be
  wrong for $b=0$. The writeup explicitly restricts to positive integers, which is
  clearly the intended reading; the classification statement should be read with
  $b\ge1$.
- **Empty subfamily.** For $l=0$ the standard convention $\bigcap_\varnothing=[k]$
  and the acyclic empty digraph agree; no issue, but the writeup does not discuss it.
- **Multi-arcs.** The paper allows parallel arcs; the writeup silently works with
  simple digraphs (digons allowed). This is WLOG since arc multiplicities never
  affect acyclicity of induced subdigraphs, and the lemma's "exactly a directed
  Hamilton cycle" is then literally correct.
- **Priority.** My web search and the catalog's 2026-05 auto-review found no prior
  resolution, but a negative literature search is never conclusive; the writeup
  itself flags this. If a prior source surfaces, the verdict would shift to
  ALREADY_KNOWN, not to any error category.
- The claim "(5,3) is a counterexample to general existence" is accurate and it is
  the smallest impossible pair.

## Referee summary

I approached this expecting the usual single-pass failure modes and found none. The
interpretation is exactly the source paper's (I verified the paper's own digon
convention and its Problem statement verbatim). The structural lemma (minimal
empty-intersection families induce exactly directed Hamilton cycles) is correct,
including the chord/reverse-arc exclusion; the $k=b+2$ impossibility via the two
overlapping minimal edge covers of $K_n$ ($\mathcal S_v$ forcing a chordless
$C_{n-1}$, $\mathcal T$ forcing a chordless $C_{n-2}$, clashing on $\mathcal R$ with
$n-5\ne n-4$ edges) is airtight for all $b\ge3$, and restriction handles $k>b+2$. All
three positive constructions are correct; I exhaustively machine-verified them
(including all 2,097,151 subfamilies for $b=2,k=7$), and I machine-verified the
$(5,3)$ impossibility by an independent finite argument that does not use the
writeup's lemma. No prior resolution was found in the literature. Verdict: CONFIRMED.
