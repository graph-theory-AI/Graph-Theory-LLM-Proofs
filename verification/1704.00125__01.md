## Interpretation

**Source statement (verified verbatim against the arXiv TeX source of 1704.00125, Section 1.2
"Limitations of our technique", lines 166–173 of `thin.tex`):**

> "It would be interesting to see whether this intuition can be made precise and for example show
> that the maximum independent set problem is APX-hard on any subgraph-closed class of graphs that
> does not have sublinear separators (or at least has exponential expansion)."

The catalog statement quotes this sentence exactly; there is no misquotation.

**Quantifier reading.** "APX-hard on any subgraph-closed class ..." must be read universally
("for every such class"), not existentially. The existential reading is trivially true and is
answered *in the same paragraph of the paper itself*: Dvořák notes MIS is APX-hard on graphs of
bounded maximum degree (citing Papadimitriou–Yannakakis), and bounded-degree graphs form a
subgraph-closed class without sublinear separators. So the only non-trivial intended reading is the
universal one, which is exactly what the writeup refutes. The writeup's formalization of "sublinear
separators" (balanced separator = vertex set whose removal leaves components of ≤ 2n/3 vertices,
sublinear = o(n) uniformly) differs cosmetically from the paper's own definition (balanced
*separations* (A,B) with |V(A)∖V(B)|, |V(B)∖V(A)| ≤ 2n/3, size |V(A)∩V(B)|; sublinear iff
lim s(n)/n = 0), but the writeup's Lemma is strong enough for both conventions: a balanced
separation of size ≤ N/12 in the constructed graph would yield sets A₀ = V(A)∖V(B),
B₀ = V(B)∖V(A) with max(|A₀|,|B₀|) ≥ 11N/24 ≥ N/3 and ≤ 2N/3, no A₀–B₀ edges, which is exactly a
partition the Lemma forbids. So s_C(N) > N/12 infinitely often and lim s(n)/n = 0 fails under
Dvořák's own definition. No convention gap.

**Is this the intended conjecture?** This is the one real point of tension, and the writeup itself
flags it honestly. Dvořák's sentence is a hedged prose research question ("whether this intuition
can be made precise and *for example* show that ..."), not a labelled conjecture. The counterexample
— MIS is exactly polynomial on bipartite graphs (König/Gallai, textbook since 1931), while
bounded-degree bipartite expanders (classical) have linear-size separators and exponential shallow-minor
expansion — is essentially folklore-level for an expert, and Dvořák very plausibly could have
produced it himself. A reasonable author would concede that the specific formalization he floated is
false as written (this is his own sentence, not a strawman distortion), but would regard the
underlying program ("make the separator/hardness intuition precise", e.g. by excluding classes where
MIS is easy for structural reasons unrelated to expansion, or by asking the question for other
problems or under extra closure hypotheses) as still open. The writeup's own verdict block and
caveats say exactly this ("would_publish: false"; "an omitted closure or richness hypothesis in the
intended question could evade this counterexample"), so the claim is not oversold.

## Reference check

The writeup is nearly self-contained; the external facts it invokes are all textbook-level, and the
one critical external object — the source problem statement — was checked against the primary source.

- **Source problem statement**: I downloaded the arXiv e-print source of 1704.00125 (`thin.tex`)
  and confirmed the open problem verbatim in Section 1.2, including the surrounding context (the
  APX-hardness-on-bounded-degree remark citing Papadimitriou–Yannakakis, and the expansion
  discussion citing Dvořák–Norin 1504.04821 and Nešetřil–Ossona de Mendez). Matches the catalog
  statement exactly.
- **Paper's definition of sublinear separators** (Section "Strongly sublinear separators" of
  `thin.tex`): balanced separations (A,B), s_G(n), lim s(n)/n = 0. Confirmed; the writeup's
  component convention is bridged in the Interpretation section — no gap.
- **König's theorem / Gallai identity / poly-time bipartite matching**: classical (König 1931,
  Gallai 1959, Hopcroft–Karp 1973); verified computationally as well.
- **APX-hardness ⟹ no PTAS unless P = NP**: standard consequence of the PCP theorem
  (Arora–Lund–Motwani–Sudan–Szegedy) plus the definition of APX-hardness under PTAS/AP-reductions.
- **Shallow minors / expansion ∇_r**: standard (Nešetřil–Ossona de Mendez, "Grad and classes with
  bounded expansion"), the very reference the source paper uses.
- **Follow-up literature**: web searches (September 2026) found no paper resolving Dvořák's
  question and no published note of this bipartite counterexample, consistent with the catalog's
  "open" status. The ingredients (bipartite expanders exist; MIS is polynomial on bipartite graphs)
  are individually classical, but I could not name a source stating the assembled counterexample,
  so the verdict is CONFIRMED rather than ALREADY_KNOWN.

## Caveats

1. **Hedged source statement.** Dvořák posed a prose research question ("whether this intuition can
   be made precise and *for example* show that ..."), not a labelled conjecture. The counterexample
   decisively falsifies the sample formalization in his sentence — his own words, universally read —
   but does not close the underlying program of finding a correct precise version (e.g. restricting
   to classes on which MIS is NP-hard, adding closure under other operations, or asking about other
   problems such as dominating set). The writeup states this caveat itself.
2. **Near-folklore content.** The counterexample combines König's theorem with the classical
   existence of bounded-degree bipartite expanders; any expert (including the author) could produce
   it. Its value as a "resolution" of the catalog item is accordingly modest, and the writeup's own
   `would_publish: false` is the right call. I nevertheless found no published statement of it.
3. **Complexity assumption.** "Not APX-hard" is meant under P ≠ NP (if P = NP, every problem in APX
   is vacuously APX-hard). Stated explicitly in the writeup.
4. **Reduction notion.** APX-hardness is reduction-dependent (PTAS-, AP-, E-reductions); under every
   standard notion, a problem solvable exactly in polynomial time cannot be APX-hard unless P = NP,
   so the conclusion is robust.
5. **Separator convention.** The writeup's component-based balanced-separator definition differs
   from the paper's separation-based one; the Lemma is strong enough for both (bridge given in the
   Interpretation section), so no repair is needed — but the writeup did not itself perform this
   bridging.
6. **Quantifier reading of "any".** Read existentially the statement would be trivially true (and
   already answered in the paper via bounded-degree classes), so the universal reading refuted here
   is the only sensible intended one.
7. **Slack in constants.** d = 20 is more than needed (any d ≥ 16 makes 5d/36 > 2 ln 3); the
   constants N/12, 2/3-balance etc. are consistent throughout, including the small cases m ≤ 5
   where |X| ≤ m/6 forces X = ∅.

## Referee summary

The writeup claims to disprove the formalized open question from Dvořák's "Thin graph classes and
polynomial-time approximation schemes" (arXiv:1704.00125, Section 1.2): that MIS is APX-hard on
every subgraph-closed class without sublinear separators (or at least with exponential expansion).
Its counterexample — bipartite graphs of maximum degree at most 20 — is correct in every detail.
The class is subgraph-closed; MIS on it is exactly polynomial by König/Gallai, hence not APX-hard
unless P = NP; a clean probabilistic argument (all constants re-derived and independently verified,
one instance checked exhaustively by computer) shows the class contains bounded-degree bipartite
graphs with no balanced separator of size N/12, under both the writeup's and the paper's separator
conventions; and an explicit tree-substitution gadget (verified by computer for r = 1,2,3) shows
∇_r ≥ 2^{r−1}, i.e. genuinely exponential expansion. Every step is VALID, the references are
textbook facts plus the primary source checked verbatim, and no published prior statement of the
counterexample was found. The only substantive reservation is interpretive, and the writeup itself
flags it: Dvořák's sentence is a hedged "for example" formalization of an intuition, and while the
refutation is decisive for the statement as written, the counterexample is folklore-grade and the
author would likely regard the broader question — a correct precise form of the
separators-versus-hardness intuition — as still open.
