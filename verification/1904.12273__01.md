## Interpretation

The catalog statement (arXiv:1904.12273, "Detecting a long odd hole", Chudnovsky-Scott-Seymour) is the
informal conjecture from the introduction of the source paper. I downloaded the v2 TeX source and the
exact passage is:

> "To generate the cleaning list in [oddholetest], we used a theorem that if $C$ is a shortest odd hole,
> and $M$ is a set of $C$-major vertices such that one of them is nonadjacent to all the others, then
> there is a ``heavy edge'' in $C$ [...]. We tried to extend this to the long odd hole situation, but
> failed. For our purposes, a ``heavy path'' of $C$ of bounded length (that is, such that every vertex
> in $M$ has a neighbour in the path) would be just as good as a heavy edge; and this extension might
> be true, but we were unable to prove it."

Points audited against the writeup's formalization:

1. **C-major definition.** The paper (source line 126) defines: $v \notin V(C)$ is $C$-major if there is
   no three-vertex path of $C$ containing all the neighbours of $v$ in $V(C)$. This is *exactly* the
   definition the writeup uses. (I checked this because the long-hole setting could plausibly have used
   a different, length-parameterized notion of major; it does not.)
2. **"Bounded length".** All bounds in the paper are explicit functions of the fixed threshold $\ell$
   (e.g. path-set costs $5\ell+12$, $16\ell+28$). The writeup's reading — a bound $b(t)$ depending only
   on the threshold — is the only reasonable one, and the counterexample kills any such bound.
3. **"Has a neighbour in the path".** The intro's heavy-path notion is literally "every vertex in $M$
   has a neighbour in the path", which is what the writeup refutes. The paper's later technical
   "catch" notion (neighbour in the *interior*, vertex off the path) differs by an additive constant;
   I computed the minimum under both notions ($q$ and $q+2$ respectively) — unbounded either way.
4. **Hidden hypotheses (the decisive audit).** The original heavy-edge theorem (arXiv:1903.00208,
   theorem `heavyedge`) reads: "Let $G$ be a graph containing no jewel or pyramid or 5-hole, and let
   $C$ be a shortest odd hole in $G$. Let $X$ be a set of $C$-major vertices, and let $x_0\in X$ be
   nonadjacent to all other members of $X$. Then there is an $X$-heavy edge in $C$." The long-hole
   paper works throughout in a *candidate*: a graph with no long pyramid, no long jewel of order at
   most $\ell+2$, and no long odd hole of length at most $2\ell+2$ (source line 359). A maximally
   hostile referee would therefore demand that the counterexample be a candidate — otherwise the
   writeup would only refute an unconditioned strawman. **I verified computationally that for
   $\ell = 7$ and odd $q \ge 9$ the counterexample graph $G(q)$ IS a candidate** (no long pyramid at
   all, no long jewel of order $\le \ell+2$, and its only odd holes are 5-holes — not long — and $C$
   itself, of length $3q > 2\ell+2$). Note the long-hole setting *cannot* exclude 5-holes (they are
   not long and carry no information about long odd holes), so the presence of 5-holes through $x$ is
   legitimate, and is in fact the structural reason the extension fails while the $t=5$ heavy-edge
   theorem (which assumes no 5-hole) survives. The counterexample also does not contradict anything
   the paper proves: the paper's replacement lemma produces a bounded *set* of paths (three short
   paths suffice in $G(q)$) with a "carefully chosen" exceptional vertex; here the exceptional vertex
   $x$ is complete to $C$, exactly the pathological choice their workaround avoids.

Hence a reasonable author of the original paper would consider the conjecture genuinely resolved in
the negative (for every fixed $\ell \ge 6$; see Caveats for $\ell = 5$). The interpretation is fair,
indeed the writeup's refutation is robust under a stronger reading than it itself claimed.

## Caveats

1. **$\ell = 5$ (i.e. $t=5$) is not refuted.** For threshold 5 the 5-holes through $x$ are themselves
   long odd holes, so $C$ is not a shortest long odd hole and the construction breaks — as it must,
   since the heavy-edge theorem is true there. The writeup states this scope limit honestly. The
   conjecture, as a statement about "the long odd hole situation" (arbitrary fixed $\ell$), is dead.
2. **Candidacy needs $q \ge \ell+2$.** The writeup allows "arbitrary odd $q\ge3$", but $G(q)$ is a
   candidate only once $q+1 > \ell+2$ (e.g. $q=7$, $\ell=7$ fails via an order-8 long jewel).
   Harmless, since the refutation only needs arbitrarily large $q$, but a fully belt-and-braces
   writeup would have said so; the writeup's own caveat ("omitted source-paper hypotheses could alter
   the conclusion") is closed in its favour by this review.
3. **Catching convention.** "Neighbour in the path" (intro/heavy-path notion, used by the writeup) vs
   "neighbour in the interior, vertex off the path" (paper's later `catch`): minima $q$ vs $q+2$;
   unbounded either way, so the verdict is convention-independent.
4. **$M$ is not stable** ($y$'s form a triangle) — permitted: both the original heavy-edge theorem
   and the conjecture only require one exceptional vertex nonadjacent to all others, which $x$
   satisfies. A weaker conjecture restricted to stable $M$ would not be touched by this example
   (for stable $M$ the paper's own machinery gets close to a positive answer), but that is not the
   stated conjecture.
5. Minor internal inconsistency: verdict `one_line` says "threshold at least seven"; the body proves
   it for all thresholds $\ge 6$. Both supported by the census.
6. The exceptional vertex $x$ is complete to $C$ — an extreme configuration, but nothing in the
   conjecture excludes it, and the paper's own hedge ("we could only prove this when the exceptional
   vertex of $M$ was carefully chosen") suggests the authors were aware this is exactly the danger
   case.

## Referee summary

I set out assuming the writeup was wrong and failed to break it. The construction is correct in every
detail: an exhaustive chordless-cycle census (independent code, $q=3$ through $11$) reproduces the
writeup's hole classification exactly, confirms $C$ is the *unique* odd hole of length $\ge 6$, and
confirms the minimum single subpath of $C$ meeting the neighbourhoods of all of
$M = \{x, y_1, y_2, y_3\}$ has exactly $q$ edges, which is unbounded for fixed threshold. The
definitions used (C-major, bounded, heavy path) match the source paper verbatim. The one attack
surface — that the intended conjecture carries the standing hypotheses of the paper's algorithmic
setting (candidate graphs; cf. the "no jewel or pyramid or 5-hole" hypotheses of the original
heavy-edge theorem) — fails too: for $q \ge 9$ the counterexample graph is itself a candidate for
$\ell = 7$ (verified by exhaustive long-jewel and long-pyramid searches with validated finders), and
the example is fully consistent with everything the paper actually proves. The heavy path extension
is refuted for every fixed threshold $\ell \ge 6$; only the (already-settled, and structurally
different) $\ell = 5$ case is untouched.
