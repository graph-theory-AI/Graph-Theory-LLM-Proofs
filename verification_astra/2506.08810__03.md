## Interpretation

**Conjecture as the source states it.** I pulled the LaTeXML rendering of arXiv:2506.08810v3
(`https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08810v3`) and extracted Section 8. Conjecture 24 reads, verbatim:

> **Conjecture 24.** Let $T$ be a finite tournament. If $T$ is not transitive, then there exists a
> countably infinite $T$-free tournament $S_T$ such that every locally finite perturbation of $S_T$
> has an induced copy of $T$.

This is character-for-character the statement in `prompt.md`. The paper's definition of the
operative term, also verbatim (Section 2, "Locally finite perturbation"):

> For a pair of distinct vertices $u,v\in V(G)$, perturbing the pair $uv$ is the operation which
> removes $uv$ from $E(G)$ if it is present and adds it if it is not present. A locally finite
> perturbation of $G$ is a graph obtained from $G$ by perturbing **arbitrarily many pairs (at least
> one, possibly infinitely many)** under the constraint that for any vertex $v$, the number of
> perturbed pairs involving $v$ is finite.

and, in the sentence introducing Problem 23, "For tournaments the natural operation is to reverse
the direction of edges".

So the intended tournament reading is exactly: a nonempty set of reversed arcs, each vertex in
finitely many reversed pairs. **This is precisely the writeup's reading.** Three consequences:

1. The writeup's caveat "perturbations are understood to be nonempty" is not an assumption it
   imposed — it is the paper's own convention ("at least one"). Good; had the writeup instead
   exploited the *empty* perturbation it would have been a strawman, and it does not.
2. "Induced copy of $T$" in a tournament is just "subtournament", so there is no induced/weak
   ambiguity to game.
3. The conjecture has no order threshold, no primeness hypothesis, no connectivity hypothesis and
   no "sufficiently large $T$" clause. A five-vertex nontransitive $T$ is squarely in scope. I
   looked for any such hidden hypothesis and there is none; the only hypothesis is "$T$ is not
   transitive", and $T_\ast$ is not transitive (verified, step 1).

**The instructed question — why would the authors have missed a five-vertex counterexample?**
Three answers, all of which I think are the real ones rather than an artifact:

- Conjecture 24 is a *speculation in the concluding open-problems section of a paper about graphs*,
  introduced with the words "Perhaps the following analogue of Theorem 1 is true for the 'locally
  finite perturbation' variant of this question." It is not presented as a tested conjecture, and
  nothing in the paper suggests the authors ran any small-tournament check. (The paper does ship
  ancillary code, `small_graphs.py` and `theorem_10.py`, but both are for the *graph* results.)
- The natural transfer of intuition from Theorem 1 is "the only obstruction is the degenerate one",
  i.e. cliques/independent sets become transitive tournaments. That guess is wrong for a reason
  that is specific to tournaments: the obstruction is not that $T_\ast$ is hard to *hit*, it is that
  $T_\ast$ is so easy to *avoid* that the whole space of countable $T_\ast$-free tournaments
  collapses onto locally-finite perturbations of the transitive order — and the transitive order is
  itself $T_\ast$-free, so the space has no isolated point at all.
- Their machinery blows vertices up into infinite cliques / infinite independent sets. The
  tournament analogue is blowing up into infinite *transitive* blocks — and $T_\ast=C_3[TT_2,TT_2,1]$
  is built so that a transitive block of size 2 sitting inside a $C_3$ already *is* a copy of
  $T_\ast$. The technique cannot get off the ground for this particular target.

A reasonable author of Conjecture 24 would, I believe, accept this as a genuine refutation of the
conjecture as posed (not of Problem 23, the single-arc variant, which the writeup explicitly does
not claim and which remains open).

**Retry-specific check.** The prompt carried an earlier `gpt-5.6-sol` attempt claiming `partial`
(the conjecture holds for inversion-number-one tournaments, for $C_5$, and for prime 3-vertex-strong
tournaments). The new writeup inherits nothing from it — correctly, since nothing is reusable — and
I checked that the two are **not in contradiction**, which is a real consistency hazard here:
$\iota(T_\ast)=2$ (computed exhaustively over all 120 orderings), $T_\ast$ has the nontrivial modules
$\{a_0,a_1\}$ and $\{b_0,b_1\}$ so it is not prime, and $T_\ast-\{c\}$ is not strongly connected so it
is not 3-vertex-strong. Hence none of the earlier attempt's positive results covers $T_\ast$. It
falls exactly into the region that attempt flagged as untreated in its own Section 8 ("low-connectivity
tournaments with nontrivial modular structure"). Had $\iota(T_\ast)$ been 1, one of the two documents
would have to be wrong; it is 2.

**1. The object (step 1).** $T_\ast$ is a tournament; outdegrees $(a_0,a_1,b_0,b_1,c)=(3,2,2,2,1)$;

**3. Lemma 3 (step 2) — also complete.** 3-vertex configurations with two $P$-empty out-arcs at a

## Caveats

1. **Nonempty perturbations.** The disproof needs "perturbation $\Rightarrow$ at least one reversed
   pair", otherwise Case 1 would be vacuous when $S$ is already transitive. This is not an
   assumption the writeup smuggled in: it is the paper's own definition. Correctly handled, and
   Case 2 exists precisely to cover the transitive $S$.
2. **Problem 23 is untouched.** The writeup says so explicitly and it is right: Case 1 in general
   reverses infinitely many arcs, so nothing here bears on the single-arc question
   ("is there a $T_\ast$-free countable tournament in which reversing any *one* arc creates
   $T_\ast$?"). A reader skimming only the verdict line could over-read the scope; the writeup's own
   `caveats` field states it.
3. **Choice.** Lemma 1's sufficiency uses a linear extension of a partial order (Szpilrajn) and
   choices of enumerations. For countable $V(S)$ this is eliminable by an explicit construction; the
   writeup neither names Szpilrajn nor remarks on this. Cosmetic.
4. **Lemma 1's necessity direction is dead weight** — correct, but never invoked. Harmless.
5. **The writeup proposes no repaired conjecture.** It disproves Conjecture 24 as posed but does not
   discuss what the right hypothesis should be (e.g. restricting to prime tournaments, or to
   tournaments with no "$P$-empty-arc" collapse, or replacing "nontransitive" by something stronger).
   Given the step-5 finding that $T_\ast$ is the unique small tournament of its kind, the natural
   follow-up — "for which $T$ does Conjecture 24 hold?" — is wide open and the writeup does not
   claim otherwise. This is an expository gap, not a mathematical one.
6. **Small-case edge behaviour.** Nothing degenerate: $T_\ast$ has 5 vertices, $S$ is countably
   infinite by hypothesis so it has arcs, and Lemma 1 is applied only to countable $S$. There is no
   $k=0/1$, empty-graph or disconnectedness edge case in play. Directed triangles are counted
   unambiguously (a triangle "containing the arc $x\to y$" $\leftrightarrow$ an element of
   $Z(x,y)$), so there is no off-by-one hiding in the definition of $J(S)$.
7. **Verdict-vs-proof match.** The one-line verdict says "every countable tournament avoiding it has
   a distinct locally finite perturbation that still avoids it" — that is exactly the Theorem in §1
   and exactly what is proved. No overclaim.

## Referee summary

I set out to find the error and could not. The interpretation is not merely defensible, it is the
paper's own: I pulled Conjecture 24 and the definition of "locally finite perturbation" verbatim
from arXiv:2506.08810v3 and both match the writeup's reading, including the "at least one" clause
that the writeup flags as a caveat. The proof has exactly two finite engines and both are completely
machine-checkable, so I checked them by exhaustive enumeration rather than by reading: Lemma 2's
configuration has only 4 (resp. 64) possible completions and every single one contains $T_\ast$;
Lemma 3's obstruction has only 2 completions per case and neither occurs; a transitive tournament
with one arc reversed is $T_\ast$-free for all 110 arcs of $TT_5,\dots,TT_9$; and across all
$2\,097\,152$ labelled tournaments on 7 vertices, all $434\,352$ that are $T_\ast$-free satisfy the
degree-$\le2$ conclusion (with the bound attained, so it is tight). The one genuinely infinitary
step, Lemma 1's sufficiency, is the place a single-pass proof would normally break — its "it follows
that" bullets about $C^-$ and $C^+$, and the reverse-enumeration ordering trick — so I re-derived all
of it line by line and it holds, including the point the writeup leaves implicit (that a cross-class
pair contradicting the *linear extension*, not just the poset, must lie in $J(S)$). I also probed for
the two failure modes specific to a retry target: the new writeup borrows nothing from the earlier
`gpt-5.6-sol` attempt, and — the real hazard — it does not contradict it, since $\iota(T_\ast)=2$,
$T_\ast$ is not prime and is not 3-vertex-strong, so $T_\ast$ sits exactly in the region that attempt
itself flagged as untreated. Finally, the "why did five authors miss a five-vertex counterexample"
worry has a satisfying answer on both sides: Conjecture 24 is a one-sentence speculation
("Perhaps the following analogue of Theorem 1 is true") in the open-problems section of a paper about
graphs, with no small-case verification anywhere in it; and on my side, an exhaustive scan of all
tournaments on at most 6 vertices shows $T_\ast$ is the *unique* one for which this argument works,
so it is a genuinely narrow object rather than something that should have been obvious. Verdict:
CONFIRMED, high confidence. Conjecture 24 of arXiv:2506.08810 is false, with
$C_3[TT_2,TT_2,1]$ as a counterexample; Problem 23 of the same paper remains open.
