## Interpretation

The writeup's formalisation is exactly the one in the source paper. I checked the
definitions against the arXiv v3 text of Johnston–Scott (arXiv:1812.09215):

* *Mapping from f to g* (paper, §1): "we say that a bijection φ : {0,1}^n → {0,1}^n is
  a mapping from f to g if, for every x ∈ {0,1}^n, we have f(x) = g(φ(x))."  With
  f = Dictator, g = XOR this is precisely the writeup's (1): ⊕_a φ_a(x) = x_1. The
  direction is the correct one (not the reverse).
* *k-local* (paper, §1): "the jth output bit φ_j depends on the ith input bit x_i if
  there exists x ∈ {0,1}^n such that φ_j(x) ≠ φ_j(x+e_i). If every output bit depends
  on at most k input bits, we say the map φ is k-local."  This is the writeup's
  P_a ⊆ [n], |P_a| ≤ k, and it is a *global* junta condition in the paper too. The
  writeup's self-declared caveat ("uses full-cube bijections and global k-junta
  locality") is therefore not a caveat at all: it is the paper's own setting.
* *Lipschitz* is w.r.t. Hamming distance, and on the cube the Lipschitz constant of a
  map equals its maximum edge stretch (concatenate a geodesic), so D = Lip(φ^{-1}) is
  a positive integer. Correct.
* The question, verbatim from the paper (and restated verbatim in Johnston's Oxford
  thesis, ORA uuid:970b5eb2, p. ~2260 of the text layer): "Does there exist a map φ
  from Dictator to XOR which is C-Lipschitz, where each output bit depends on at most
  k input bits and such that φ^{-1} is o(log n/log k)-Lipschitz as n,k → ∞?"

The writeup proves D ≥ 1 + log n/log k for **every** such φ, with no hypothesis on C
at all. That is strictly stronger than what is needed to answer the question, and it
answers it in the negative in the sense a reasonable author would accept. There is no
interpretation gaming: no hypothesis is strengthened, no strawman reading is refuted,
and the result is not sensitive to any convention (height/depth, indexing, the choice
of the dictator coordinate). **Interpretation: fair and intended.**

One genuine scope remark: the writeup answers the *maximum-stretch* question. It says
nothing about average stretch, and nothing about maps into a larger cube. Neither is
asked.

named and for the ones this problem invites:

* *Self-overlapping ball count* — not applicable (step 12).
* *Off-by-one in N_k* — checked three ways: by hand, against the tree vertex count, and

* *k = 1, 2 and small n* — step 20, and k = 2 gives N_2(D) = D, i.e. n ≤ D, which is

* *Silent use of linearity* — §2 uses none. The counting is purely combinatorial and
  applies verbatim to nonlinear maps; my exhaustive searches below include all

## Reference check

The writeup cites **nothing** — it invokes no external theorem, so there is no
misquoted citation to find. I nevertheless checked everything it is implicitly
positioned against, by downloading the sources.

1. **arXiv:1812.09215v3 (Johnston–Scott), definitions.** Confirmed verbatim, quoted in
   the Interpretation section above. The writeup's (1) and its locality hypothesis are
   exactly the paper's.
2. **arXiv:1812.09215v3, Theorem 1.** Confirmed verbatim: "Let φ : {0,1}^n → {0,1}^n be
   a mapping from Dictator to XOR which is C-Lipschitz and where each output bit
   depends on at most k input bits. Then there is a constant δ = Ω(1/(k + log(C))) such
   that the inverse map φ^{-1} is not δ log(n)-Lipschitz. Furthermore, if φ is a linear
   mapping, then we may take δ = Ω(1/log(C+k))."  Their proof (§2) builds one *global*
   dependency graph, bounds the left-degree by C·2^{k−1} via a uniformly random point
   (paying 2^{1−k} for each witness), and loses a further factor 2 to bipartiteness in
   their Lemma 6. The writeup's localized branching argument pays neither cost, which
   is a coherent explanation of why it improves Ω(1/(k+log C)) to the sharp Ω(1/log k).
   The new bound does **not** contradict Theorem 1; it strictly implies it.
3. **The open question.** Confirmed verbatim in arXiv:1812.09215v3 §2 and, independently,
   in Tom Johnston's Oxford DPhil thesis "Problems in Extremal and Probabilistic
   Combinatorics" (ORA uuid:970b5eb2), where it is restated word for word with no
   progress reported. So the problem really was open.
4. **arXiv:1501.03016 (Rao–Shinkar), Theorem 2 and Appendix A — PRIOR ART FOR §3.**
   This is the one substantive finding of the reference check. Rao–Shinkar's Appendix A
   constructs A = I + M where M is the adjacency matrix of a complete **binary tree**
   with the root labelled 1, proves A^{-1} is the ancestor/descendant matrix, and notes
   the resulting map is 3-local, 2-Lipschitz with O(log n)-Lipschitz inverse. They then
   write, verbatim: "Note that the above proof can be generalized to obtain a mapping φ
   that is L-local 2-Lipschitz such that φ^{-1} is C-Lipschitz, for any L and C that
   satisfy (L−1)^C ≥ n by replacing the tree G in the proof with a complete L−1-ary
   tree on n vertices."  That is *exactly* the writeup's §3 (its (12) is A = I + M and
   its (13) is A^{-1} = ancestor matrix), and Johnston–Scott cite it as the source of
   δ(2,k) = O(1/log k). The writeup presents §3 as its own with no attribution. The
   only thing new in §3 is the BFS truncation, which turns Rao–Shinkar's (k−1)^d ≥ n
   into the exact threshold n ≤ N_k(d) — a genuine but very small sharpening, and it is
   what makes the claimed *exact* optimum (3) exact rather than merely asymptotic.
5. **Follow-up literature.** Semantic Scholar lists exactly one paper citing
   arXiv:1812.09215 ("On Mappings on the Hypercube with Small Average Stretch",
   Boczkowski–Shinkar, 2019), which concerns average stretch and does not touch this
   gap. Four web searches turned up no paper resolving Ω(1/k) vs O(1/log k). I found no
   prior appearance of the lower bound n ≤ N_k(D). So the **lower bound (§2) is not
   ALREADY_KNOWN**, while the **construction (§3) is**.

## Caveats

1. **Unattributed prior art (the main defect).** §3 is Rao–Shinkar's construction
   (arXiv:1501.03016, Theorem 2 + the generalisation note in Appendix A), presented as
   if new. The writeup's own caveat field says "literature priority has not been
   independently checked", which is honest but understates it: the construction half of
   the "exact optimum" is 2015 work that the source paper explicitly cites. This does
   not affect correctness, and the *new* half — the lower bound — is, as far as I can
   determine, not in the literature.
2. **Step 3 asserted, not proved.** "The sets R_a are nonempty and pairwise distinct" is
   one line of missing argument. Trivial, but it is load-bearing for the base case.
3. **The claimed exactness of (3) is slightly stronger than the question needs** and
   depends on the BFS-truncation refinement of the construction; the asymptotic answer
   to the question does not depend on it.
4. **k = 0** is not discussed (vacuous: constant outputs are not injective). **n = 1** is
   fine (φ = id, D = 1 = N_k(1)). **k = 1** is handled correctly.
5. **Maximum stretch only.** The result says nothing about average stretch, about
   embeddings into larger cubes, or about maps from XOR to Dictator. None is asked, but
   the one-line verdict ("No") should be read as answering only the maximum-stretch
   question.
6. **Locality is the paper's global notion.** If someone instead meant "locally k-junta"
   (per-region dependence), the argument would need redoing. The paper does not mean
   that, so this is not a live objection.
7. **The one-line verdict slightly oversells §3**: "linear 2-Lipschitz tree maps attain
   the resulting bound" is true but is Rao–Shinkar's, not the writeup's, contribution.
8. **Extraordinary-claim risk.** This is a two-page elementary argument that settles a
   question posed in a CPC paper and restated in an Oxford thesis, superseding that
   paper's Theorem 1 in the general case. I verified every inference and could not
   break it, and the exhaustive searches agree with it to the last unit at every sharp
   threshold I could reach (n ≤ 6 for D = 2, n ≤ 5 for k = 2, n = 4 for k = 3). But the
   sharp threshold that would most stress the argument's *depth* recursion —
   (n,k) = (8,3), where the theorem forbids D = 3 — is out of brute-force reach, so my
   computational evidence probes the branching factor much harder than the depth.

## Referee summary

I set out to break this and failed. The load-bearing object is the Claim in §2, and its
inductive step survives pedantic re-derivation: fixing a nonempty T with T ⊆ R_a for all
a in the family, one picks a *single* output coordinate b whose value already moved
between x and x^T; every other target a must then move φ_b back, which forces R_a to
meet P_b outside T, and |P_b \ T| ≤ k−1 because P_b must already meet T. That is a
covering of A_d(T) by 1 + (k−1) smaller families, so the recursion
|A_d(T)| ≤ 1 + (k−1)|A_d(T∪{i})| is a union bound. The specific failure mode the brief
asked me to look for — a Moore-type count that silently assumes the levels of a ball are
disjoint — does not arise, precisely because this is a covering argument in which
overlap only helps. The forward constant C is genuinely never used, which is exactly
the source of the improvement over Johnston–Scott's Theorem 1: their global dependency
graph must bound the left-degree by C·2^{k−1} (a uniform-measure witness costing 2^{1−k}),
and they lose another factor 2 to bipartiteness; the localized argument pays neither, so
Ω(1/(k+log C)) becomes the sharp Ω(1/log k). Computationally the bound is not merely
true but exactly attained everywhere I could check: the tree construction hits
Lip(φ^{-1}) = d_min for all 78 pairs (n ≤ 14, k ≤ 7); an exhaustive sweep of *all* 576
Dictator→XOR bijections of Q_3, all 124416 3-local ones of Q_4, all 46080 2-local ones
of Q_5, and all maps with 2-Lipschitz inverse for n ≤ 6 reproduces min D = d_min in
every single case, with zero violations of the pointwise Claim over ~1700 maps × all
base points × all subsets T. The two real deficiencies are scholarly rather than
mathematical: §3 reproduces Rao–Shinkar's (k−1)-ary tree construction without
attribution (arXiv:1501.03016, Appendix A, which Johnston–Scott cite as the source of
δ(2,k) = O(1/log k)), so only the lower bound §2 is new, and the assertion that the R_a
are distinct is left to the reader. Subject to the usual caveat that an elementary
two-page resolution of a published open problem deserves a second human referee — and
noting that the deepest sharp case, (n,k) = (8,3), is beyond brute force — I confirm the
writeup: the answer to Johnston–Scott's question is **no**, and δ(2,k) = Θ(1/log k) for
general maps, not just linear ones.
