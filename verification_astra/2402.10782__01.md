## Interpretation

The problem (Problem 4.4 of arXiv:2402.10782, *Finding forest-orderings of tournaments is
NP-complete*, Aboulker–Aubian–Lopes) asks for the complexity of the $\mathcal C$-FAS problem
when $\mathcal C$ is the class of all paths, and when $\mathcal C$ is the class of graphs of
maximum degree $1$. The writeup uses exactly the source paper's definitions: $F\subseteq A(T)$
is a FAS if $T-F$ is acyclic, and it is a $\mathcal C$-FAS if the undirected graph induced by
its edges lies in $\mathcal C$. No hypothesis is weakened, no literal-reading trick is used,
and both halves of the question are answered. This is the intended reading and a reasonable
author of the original problem would regard it as addressed.

Two convention points, both handled honestly:

* **Paths vs linear forests.** Lemma 1 turns "has a path-FAS" into "has an ordering whose
  backward graph is a linear forest" by completing a linear forest to a *spanning* undirected
  path (possible because the underlying graph of a tournament is complete). The argument is
  correct under either reading of "the graph induced by the edges of $F$" (with or without
  isolated vertices), because the completion is Hamiltonian.
* **Tiny inputs.** Lemma 1 is stated for $n\ge2$ and the writeup explicitly defers $n\le1$ to
  "the chosen convention for an empty path". Irrelevant to the hardness claim.

Being a `attacks_retry` target, the prompt contained a previous `gpt-5.6-sol` attempt that got
the matching case and the degree-$d$ DP but stalled on paths. The new writeup re-derives that
material (Sections 1, 2, 5) rather than inheriting it, and I checked it independently; the
genuinely new content is Sections 3–4 (the $U_m$ gadget and the 3-SAT reduction). The earlier
attempt's own Section 9 correctly diagnosed why the prefix-set DP cannot be extended to
acyclicity; the new writeup does not repeat that error — it changes strategy to a reduction.

## Reference check

The writeup cites **no external results at all** — it is self-contained, so there is nothing to
misquote, and `references_ok: true` is vacuously satisfied. The problem is the opposite one:
prior art that should have been cited.

Confirmed, from primary sources:

1. **arXiv:2402.10782v4** (fetched; DMTCS 28:2, July 2026). Problem 4.4 is still stated verbatim
   as *"What is the complexity of the $\mathcal C$-FAS Problem when $\mathcal C$ is the set of all
   paths? when $\mathcal C$ is the set of graphs with maximum degree 1?"*. v4 proves nothing about
   paths or matchings, and never mentions sparse tournaments or degreewidth. So the catalog's
   "open" status was not stale at the level of the source paper.

2. **Davot, Isenmann, Roy, Thiebaut, "Degreewidth: a new parameter for solving problems on
   tournaments", arXiv:2212.06007 (Dec 2022), WG 2023** (PDF fetched and read). Abstract,
   verbatim: *"the class of sparse tournaments defined by Bessy et al. [ESA 2017] is exactly the
   class of tournaments with degreewidth one … We also provide a cubic algorithm to decide if a
   tournament is sparse."* Body, verbatim: *"A tournament is sparse if there exists an ordering
   such that the backward arcs form a matching."* By the writeup's own step 2, that is **exactly**
   the $\mathcal C$-FAS problem for $\mathcal C=$ graphs of maximum degree $1$. Hence **Theorem
   part 1 of the writeup — including its $O(n^3)$ bound — is already in the literature**, and the
   matching half of Problem 4.4 was answered four years before this attack.
   * Their **Lemma 4** is the writeup's score localization (2) specialised to $d=1$: *"If $\sigma$
     is a sparse ordering, then for any vertex $v$ such that $d^-(v)=i$, the only possible
     positions of $v$ in $\sigma$ are $\{i,i+1,i+2\}\cap[n]$"* — identical, since
     $a(v)=d^-(v)+1$.
   * Their **Definition 3** is the writeup's gadget, verbatim: *"we define $U_n$ as the tournament
     on $n$ vertices with $V(U_n)=\{v_1,\dots,v_n\}$ and
     $A(U_n)=\{(v_{i+1},v_i)\mid i\in[n-1]\}\cup\{(v_i,v_j)\mid 1\le i<n,\ i+1<j\le n\}$"* — the
     same tournament, under the same name $U_n$. And *"each $U$-tournament of order $n>4$ has
     exactly two sparse orderings"* is precisely the writeup's **Lemma 2**, which the writeup
     announces as *"The new ingredient is a two-state gadget"*. It is a rediscovery.

3. **Keeney, Lokshtanov, "Degreewidth on semi-complete digraphs", WG 2024, LNCS
   10.1007/978-3-031-75409-8_22.** Degreewidth of a semi-complete digraph computable in
   $\Delta^{O(\Delta)}n+O(n^2)$; FAS and Cutwidth are FPT in degreewidth. This subsumes the
   writeup's Section 5 (its $2^{O(d)}n^{O(1)}$ DP), with a better-known pedigree; the writeup's
   $2^{4d}n^3$ is a cleaner bound but not a new theorem.

4. **Aboulker, Oijid, Petit, Rocton, Simon, "Computing the degreewidth of a digraph is hard",
   arXiv:2407.19270, DMTCS (accepted Feb 2026)** (PDF fetched). Its survey paragraph confirms 2
   and 3 verbatim: *"for tournaments with $n$ vertices, it is proved in Davot et al. (2023) that
   it is NP-hard to compute Degreewidth, that sorting by in-degree gives a 3-approximation, and
   that one can decide if an $n$-vertex tournament has degreewidth $\le1$ in $O(n^3)$ steps."*
   Its own hardness ($k$-Degreewidth NP-complete for every $k\ge1$) is for **oriented graphs**,
   not tournaments, so it does **not** contradict the writeup's Section 5. Note that Pierre
   Aboulker is an author of both this paper and the source paper.

5. No follow-up resolving the **path** case was found: searches over 2026 math.CO/cs.DM
   listings, the citing literature of 2402.10782, and the degreewidth line of work turned up
   nothing on path-FAS / linear-forest FAS in tournaments.

**Self-contamination check (2c).** All prior art named above is independent of this campaign:

al. is ESA 2017; Keeney–Lokshtanov is WG 2024. I read the actual PDFs, not search-engine

## Computational check

Scripts (mine, plus the surviving ones from the killed earlier run) are in
`verification_astra/scripts/2402.10782__01/`. All use `python3` only.

* `struct_check.py` — independent re-implementation of the whole Section-4 reduction, plus a
  **structured exhaustive** decision procedure for "does $T$ have an ordering whose backward
  graph is a linear forest?". The naive search blows up because the padding and private
  vertices permute freely; the search here quotients that out by (i) confining every vertex to
  its exact $d=2$ score window, and (ii) memoising on the pair *(prefix set, partition of the
  still-extendable vertices into current components)* — sound because, once $v$ is placed, all of
  its backward edges are determined by the prefix **set**, and a placed $u$ can still gain edges
  iff some unplaced $w$ has $w\to u$, again a function of the prefix set. The procedure is
  **complete**: any linear-forest ordering has $\Delta\le2$, hence respects the windows.
  - `check_localization`: 4000 random tournaments, identity (2) never violated.
  - `(x\lor y\lor z)`: $n=60$, $\Delta(B_\sigma)=3$, min wire span 16; ordering found.
  - 7 of the 8 clauses on $x,y,z$ (unique model): $n=152$; ordering found.
  - **all 8 clauses on $x,y,z$ (UNSAT)**: $n=170$; exhaustively **no** linear-forest ordering —
    1111 search nodes, 294 memo hits, 817 states, 0.2 s.
* `validate.py` — (a) the structured solver agrees with a full $n!$ brute force on **400 random
  tournaments**, $n\le8$ (387 yes-instances, **0 mismatches**), which validates completeness of
  the search; (b) 40 random 3-CNFs run through the reduction, 0 mismatches with satisfiability;
  (c) the Section-4.6 promise: the all-false ordering always has $\Delta(B_\pi)=2$, including on
  UNSAT instances.
* `extra.py` — (i) Lemma 2 in exactly the form step 4.4 needs, for every gadget size actually
  used: enumerating *all* $\Delta\le1$ orderings of $U_m$ gives exactly 2 backward graphs
  $\{M_0,M_1\}$ for $m=5,7,9,11,15,21,23,27,29,31,33,41$; (ii) every wire of every instance is
  forced backward by **disjoint** $d=2$ windows (stronger than the writeup's "moves by $\le5$"
  argument), with $\Delta(B_\sigma)=3$ and min wire span $16$–$43$; (iii) six genuinely UNSAT
  formulas (up to $n=272$, 112 wires) all exhaustively refuted: **no path-FAS**, in $\le0.7$ s
  each.
* `gadget_check.py` (earlier run) — brute force over *all* matchings of $K_m$: the only matching
  FASs of $U_m$ are $M_0,M_1$, for $m=5,7,9,11$.
* `dp_check.py`, `dp_check2.py` (earlier run) — the Section-5 DP matches full $n!$ brute force on
  $3\times720$ random tournaments for $d=0,1,2$ and on engineered NO-instances for $d=2,3$.

## Caveats

1. **Novelty, and only novelty, is the problem.** Theorem part 1 is Davot et al. (2023);
   Lemma 2 (announced as "the new ingredient") is their U-tournament lemma; (2) is their Lemma 4;
   Section 5 is Keeney–Lokshtanov (WG 2024). The writeup's own caveat block says novelty "has not
   been independently checked" — it should have been. The `would_publish: true` flag is not
   safe as the writeup stands.
2. **What does survive as new** (and is, on this review, correct): the NP-completeness of
   path-FAS, i.e. the reduction of Sections 4.1–4.6. It answers the path half of Problem 4.4.
   Rewritten with citations — "using the U-tournaments of Davot et al., we show …" — it is a
   genuine contribution. Nothing in the reduction depends on the parts that are already known
   being new.
3. $n\le1$ is waved through ("the chosen convention for an empty path"). Harmless, but a
   referee would want it stated.
4. Lemma 2 is *false* for $m=3,4$ (Davot et al. record three sparse orderings for each). The
   writeup only ever uses odd $m\ge5$, so this is not a bug, but the hypothesis is load-bearing
   and the writeup states it only in passing.
5. The reduction is generous with padding (12 vertices per block gap, $\ge13$ separation for a
   bound that needs $\ge11$) and with buffers; nothing breaks, but the instance is bigger than
   necessary. Irrelevant to correctness.
6. The contrast the verdict advertises ("in contrast with a maximum-degree variant") is genuinely
   established, not merely asserted — but it is established by re-proving a known theorem.
7. Both halves rest on the standard *deletion* definition of a FAS; under a *reversal* definition
   the reduction would need re-checking. The writeup flags this.

## Referee summary

I could not break this proof. Both directions of the correctness equivalence are correct, NP
membership is immediate, the reduction is polynomial, the source problem is NP-hard in the form
used, and every finite object — the $U_m$ gadget, the wire matching, $\Delta(B_\sigma)=3$, the
wire spans, the Section-4.5 ordering — survives independent implementation. More than that, a
*structured exhaustive* search (score windows plus memoisation on prefix set and component
partition, validated against full $n!$ brute force on 400 small tournaments) decides the path-FAS
question outright on real reduction instances with up to 272 vertices, and the equivalence
"satisfiable $\iff$ path-FAS" held on every one, including six genuinely unsatisfiable formulas.

The finding is therefore not an error but a priority problem, and it is a large one. The
writeup's Theorem has two halves. The second — path-FAS is NP-complete — is new as far as I can
establish and is correct. The first — matching-FAS is decidable in $O(n^3)$ — is verbatim a
2022 theorem: matching-FAS *is* the recognition of Bessy et al.'s sparse tournaments, i.e.
degreewidth $\le1$, and Davot, Isenmann, Roy and Thiebaut give a cubic algorithm for exactly
that (arXiv:2212.06007, WG 2023), as the 2026 DMTCS survey by Aboulker, Oijid, Petit, Rocton and
Simon restates verbatim. Worse for the writeup's rhetoric, the gadget it advertises as "the new
ingredient" is the same authors' $U_n$ tournament under the same name, with the same "exactly
two sparse orderings" lemma, and the score-localization inequality is their Lemma 4; Section 5's
DP is subsumed by Keeney and Lokshtanov (WG 2024). I record ALREADY_KNOWN because the claimed
verdict announces the matching result as a finding and because a load-bearing lemma is a
rediscovery — but the campaign should not discard this target: with the prior art cited and part
1 demoted to a citation, the path-FAS reduction is a correct, self-contained resolution of half
of Problem 4.4 and is worth writing up.
